Who controls it/10 of 13

Who decides what is allowed

Somebody chooses the rules for what can be said and shown, and that somebody is a company rather than a government, a court, or you.

How old is your kid?

How to explain it, by age

Same idea every time. What changes is how much of the machinery a kid can hold, so pick the version that fits and go up a band when it stops being interesting.

Ages 4-6

Use rules, which she already thinks about constantly. "Every game has rules about what you are allowed to do. Somebody wrote those rules. They are not the same as the rules at home or at school."

Try this

Ask her what rule she would add to a game she plays, and who she thinks she would tell.

Listen for

Her understanding that a person made the rule. That is all this needs at this age.

If she already has more access than most. Show her the report button in something she uses and explain what it does.

Ages 7-9

Introduce the company. "The app has rules about what people can post, and the company that owns the app writes them. If something breaks the rules it can be taken down. Sometimes a computer decides that, and computers get it wrong quite often in both directions."

Try this

Find the community rules page for something she uses and read three of them together. They are usually short, vague, and much more interesting than she expects.

Listen for

Her spotting a rule that seems unclear or unfair. That instinct is correct and worth encouraging.

If she already has more access than most. Ask what she would do if something she posted were removed by mistake, and whether she knows how to appeal.

Ages 10-12

Introduce scale and its consequences. "Millions of posts arrive every hour, so nobody can read them all. Automated systems make almost all the calls, and they are wrong constantly, in both directions: things that should stay get removed, things that should go stay up. And the rules differ by country, because different governments demand different things."

Try this

Look up a case where a platform removed something famously wrongly. There are many and they are easy to find. Ask her how she would have decided.

Listen for

Her recognising that there is no rule set that gets every case right. That is a genuinely mature conclusion.

If she already has more access than most. Introduce the fact that the same post can be legal in one country and illegal in another, and that the company has to pick.

Ages 13+

Go to the power question. A small number of companies now make speech rules for billions of people, with limited transparency and little external accountability. Whether that is better or worse than governments doing it is a serious argument with serious people on both sides. Underneath it sits a workforce, often outsourced to countries where labour is cheap, reviewing the worst material humans produce.

Try this

Have him read one platform's transparency report and one independent account of the same period. The gap between the two is the exercise.

Listen for

Him identifying that both "the companies should decide" and "governments should decide" have obvious failure modes, and resisting the urge to pick a side too quickly.

If she already has more access than most. Introduce oversight boards, the Digital Services Act, and the question of who should be able to compel a company to reinstate something.

Why this concept matters more than it looks

Most media literacy stops at “think critically about what you see”. This one goes a step behind that: who arranged for you to be able to see it at all.

A child who asks who is in charge here has a question that keeps working everywhere, on platforms that do not exist yet, in arguments about regulation they will vote on one day.

Resist the tidy answer

There is no arrangement of this that is obviously right, and pretending otherwise is the one thing likely to make a teenager stop listening.

Companies deciding is bad, because they are unaccountable and motivated by profit. Governments deciding is bad in a different way, and considerably worse in some countries. Nobody deciding produces a place most people will not use.

Sitting in that discomfort honestly is the lesson. A child who can hold a genuine dilemma without collapsing it into a slogan is doing the actual work of thinking critically.

Misconceptions to address

  • That the rules are the law. Platform rules are company policy, they differ between companies, and they change without notice.
  • That removal decisions are made carefully one by one. Most are made by automated systems at enormous volume, with human review reserved for appeals and high-profile cases.
  • That being banned from a platform is censorship in the legal sense. It is a company enforcing its own rules, which is a different argument from the free speech one, though a real argument remains about how much power that gives a handful of firms.
  • Adults frequently assume someone official is checking. In most cases nobody is.

Running this with a class

Have the class write the rules for a school social network: what is allowed, who decides, what happens on a breach, how you appeal. Then hand them ten genuinely difficult edge cases and make them apply their own rules. Every group discovers their rules were too vague, which is exactly the real problem.
