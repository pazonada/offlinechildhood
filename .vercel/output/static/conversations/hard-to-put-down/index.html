How it works/6 of 13

Designed to be hard to put down

The difficulty you feel stopping was engineered by people who are very good at their jobs. It is not a flaw in you.

How to explain it, by age

Same idea every time. What changes is how much of the machinery a kid can hold, so pick the version that fits and go up a band when it stops being interesting.

Ages 4-6

Point at one thing. "See how the next one starts by itself before you can decide? Somebody made it do that on purpose, so it is harder to stop." One feature, named out loud, is plenty.

Try this

Before watching, agree on two episodes. When the third starts by itself, pause it and say: that is the bit we talked about. Naming it in the moment is what makes it stick.

Listen for

Her spotting the autoplay herself next time. Spotting is the whole skill at this age.

If the child already has more access than most: Let her press stop herself, so the stopping is something she does rather than something done to her.

Ages 7-9

Compare it to a fruit machine. "It never really ends. There is always one more, and you never know if the next one will be great, so it is hard to stop looking. Sweet shops put sweets at the till for the same reason. People are paid to work out what makes you keep going."

Try this

Find three features together that exist to keep you going: autoplay, the streak, the pull to refresh. Make it a hunt. Three is enough for a pattern to appear.

Listen for

Her identifying a feature you did not mention. That is the skill transferring.

If the child already has more access than most: Have her turn one off and report back on whether she missed it. Usually she will not, which is the interesting part.

Ages 10-12

Name the mechanism properly. "The strongest version is unpredictable rewards. If every post were good you would get bored. If none were you would leave. Because it is unpredictable, you keep checking, and that is the most compulsive pattern known in psychology. It is the same one that makes gambling work, and it is not an accident that it is in your feed."

Try this

Have her turn off all notifications for a week and note when she reaches for the phone anyway. The reaching without a prompt is the finding, and it is more convincing coming from her own week than from you.

Listen for

Her separating "I want this" from "this was built to make me want it". Holding both is the mature version.

If the child already has more access than most: Introduce the fact that the people who build these products often restrict them heavily for their own children, and ask what she makes of that.

Ages 13+

Move past blame. Most individual designers are not trying to harm anyone; they are optimising a metric they were handed, and compulsion is what falls out of maximising time-on-app. Then get concrete about what actually helps, because willpower mostly does not: changing the environment does. Where the phone sits, what is installed, what can send a notification.

Try this

Have him track his own usage for a week without changing anything, then predict the number before looking. The gap between the estimate and the reality is the lesson, and it is the same gap adults have.

Listen for

Him proposing an environmental change instead of promising to try harder. That is the transfer worth having.

If the child already has more access than most: Discuss why regulation of these design patterns is being debated, and what a rule that actually worked would have to say.

Say it is not their fault, and mean it

The most valuable sentence in this whole concept is that the difficulty is not a personal failing.

Children are told constantly to have more self-control about screens, and they mostly cannot, because the thing was built by well-resourced teams specifically to defeat that. Being told to try harder against a professional adversary you cannot see is demoralising, and it produces concealment sooner than restraint.

Naming the design does something different. It makes the difficulty external, which makes it discussable, which is the only route to a kid telling you when something has got out of hand.

Then change the environment

Once the mechanism is named, skip the willpower and change the setup: fewer prompts, more friction. Notifications off, phone in another room, the app removed from the home screen.

This is also why deciding where devices live does more work than any rule about minutes. It changes the environment rather than asking a child to win a fight nobody wins.

Misconceptions to address

  • ✕

    That struggling to stop means weak willpower. Teams of skilled people worked to produce that exact effect, and it works on adults too.

  • ✕

    That the techniques are hidden. Most are visible once named, which is what makes this concept immediately useful.

  • ✕

    That deleting one app solves it. The same patterns are in the next one, because they work.

  • ✕

    Adults blame children for a difficulty they demonstrably share.

Running this with a class

Give small groups the brief of designing an app to keep a class of ten-year-olds using it as long as possible. They will invent streaks, notifications and infinite scroll within ten minutes. Then show them those features in real products. Having designed the trap themselves, they never look at it the same way.
