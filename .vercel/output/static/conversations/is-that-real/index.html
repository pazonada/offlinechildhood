Risks and guardrails/8 of 11

Is that real?

A photograph is no longer evidence that something happened. Working out what is real is now a skill rather than something you can see.

How old is your kid?

How to explain it, by age

Same idea every time. What changes is how much of the machinery a kid can hold, so pick the version that fits and go up a band when it stops being interesting.

Ages 4-6

Introduce made-up pictures, which she already understands from drawing. "Computers can make pictures of things that never happened. They can look like photographs. If a picture looks strange or scary, come and ask me."

Try this

Generate a picture of something impossible but friendly, like your cat driving a bus. Making a fake together removes the mystery and the fear at the same time.

Listen for

Her asking whether a picture is real. The question itself is the goal.

If she already has more access than most. Add that a video can be made up too, which is less obvious to a child than a picture.

Ages 7-9

Extend to video and voice. "It can make videos of people saying things they never said, and copy someone's voice from a few seconds of recording. So seeing something is no longer proof it happened. The way you check is where it came from, not what it looks like."

Try this

Take one photograph she has seen shared and trace it back to who first posted it. Doing the tracing once builds the habit better than being told to check.

Listen for

Her asking who posted it and where they got it, rather than studying the image.

If she already has more access than most. Introduce reverse image search as a practical tool she can use herself.

Ages 10-12

Move the whole question from looking to sourcing. "You will not out-look this. The fakes improve every few months and the tells stop working. What does keep working is asking where it came from, whether anyone reliable is also reporting it, and who benefits from you believing it. That last one catches a surprising amount."

Try this

Take something that made her angry or delighted online this week and trace it to an original source together. Strong feeling is the signal to check, and connecting those two things is the habit worth building.

Listen for

Her noticing her own reaction and treating it as a prompt to verify.

If she already has more access than most. Introduce the reverse problem: real footage dismissed as fake, which is increasingly the more common tactic.

Ages 13+

Name the deeper consequence, sometimes called the liar's dividend. Once everyone knows anything can be faked, genuine evidence can be waved away as fabricated. The damage is not only that false things get believed. It is that true things stop being provable, and that erosion is harder to repair than any individual fake.

Try this

Have him look at how newsrooms and organisations such as WITNESS verify material: provenance, metadata, corroboration, geolocation. It is investigative work rather than perception, and seeing the actual method is clarifying.

Listen for

Him articulating why "you cannot trust anything" is the wrong conclusion and a genuinely dangerous one.

If she already has more access than most. Discuss content provenance standards and why cryptographic signing of camera output is being pursued as a partial answer.

Teach the habit, not the tells

Every article about spotting fakes lists visual giveaways. Those lists are obsolete within months, and worse, they leave a child confident in a skill that has quietly stopped working.

The durable skill is provenance. Where did this come from? Who first published it? Is anyone reliable saying the same thing? Who gains if I believe it?

Those questions worked before synthetic media existed and will keep working after, because they are about the chain of custody rather than the pixels.

Do not land on total distrust

The failure mode is a teenager who concludes that nothing can be known and therefore nothing is worth checking. That is more useful to a liar than credulity, and it is where a lot of “do your own research” ends up.

The goal is a child who can hold two things at once: that fakery is cheap and common, and that some sources are much more reliable than others, and that telling them apart is work worth doing.

Misconceptions to address

  • That you can spot fakes by looking closely. Counting fingers worked briefly and does not any more. Any list of visual tells has a short shelf life.
  • That only video and photographs are affected. Voice is the easiest to fake and the most convincing, because we have no habit of doubting it.
  • That this is only about famous people. The cheapest harm is ordinary: a fake voice note, a fabricated screenshot of a group chat, an image of a classmate.
  • Adults are considerably worse at this than teenagers, and considerably more confident.

Running this with a class

Show four images, two real and two generated, and have the class vote before revealing. Run it again a month later with fresh examples and compare the class accuracy. Watching their own detection rate fail to improve is the lesson: the answer is provenance, not perception.
