Risks and guardrails/9 of 13

Who is actually on the other end

Online, you cannot tell who you are talking to, and increasingly you cannot tell whether it is a person at all.

How old is your kid?

How to explain it, by age

Same idea every time. What changes is how much of the machinery a kid can hold, so pick the version that fits and go up a band when it stops being interesting.

Ages 4-6

Keep it simple and non-frightening. "In games and videos you cannot see who you are talking to. So we only talk to people we know in real life. And if anyone asks you to keep a secret from me, that is when you come and tell me."

Try this

Agree the secrets rule explicitly and repeat it occasionally. Surprises are fine, secrets from parents are not. It is a small distinction that carries a great deal.

Listen for

Her repeating the secrets rule back without prompting.

If she already has more access than most. If she plays anything with chat, sit with her while she plays it and see what the chat actually looks like.

Ages 7-9

Introduce the unverifiable. "Anyone can say they are anyone. A person in a game saying they are nine could be anyone at all. Nobody checks. That is not to frighten you, it is just how it works, so we stick to people we actually know."

Try this

Make a fake profile together, in five minutes, with a borrowed photograph and an invented age. Seeing how easy it is beats any warning.

Listen for

Her saying you cannot know for sure. Uncertainty is the concept, not fear.

If she already has more access than most. Add the rule that moving a conversation to a private app is itself the warning sign, whoever suggests it.

Ages 10-12

Teach the techniques rather than the scams. "The specific tricks change constantly. What does not change is the pressure. Urgency, so you cannot think. Secrecy, so you cannot check. Flattery, so you feel special. Authority, so you do not question. If you feel any of those, that is the signal to stop and ask someone."

Try this

Go through a real scam message together and label the techniques in it. Doing it on a real example makes the pattern portable.

Listen for

Her naming a technique rather than describing the scam. That is what transfers to the version she has not seen yet.

If she already has more access than most. Introduce sextortion plainly and age-appropriately, along with the single most important fact: it ends when an adult is told, and nobody sensible blames the child.

Ages 13+

Add that the other end may not be a person. Convincing conversational systems are cheap now, and they are used at scale for romance scams, influence and support-line fraud. Then say the thing that matters more than any of it: if something goes wrong, the way out is telling someone, and being tricked is not shameful. Skilled adults get caught by this professionally.

Try this

Have him look at how a documented romance or sextortion scam actually unfolded, step by step. The methodical patience of it is what surprises people and what makes it recognisable later.

Listen for

Him saying he would tell you, and believing it. That is the only outcome here that genuinely protects him.

If she already has more access than most. Discuss why shame is the mechanism these scams depend on, and how removing the shame takes away most of their power.

The rule that matters more than the rest

One thing on this page does more work than everything else: your child has to believe that telling you will make it better rather than worse.

Every one of these harms depends on silence. Sextortion depends on it completely. The scam only works while the child thinks they will be blamed, punished, or have their devices taken away.

Which means the most protective thing you can do is boring. Say, repeatedly and in advance, that if anything goes wrong they can tell you and you will not be angry and you will not confiscate anything as a punishment. Then, when something small goes wrong, prove it by not being angry. The small incidents are the rehearsal for the big one.

Why fear does not work here

Frightening children about strangers has been tried extensively and it has two problems. It aims attention at the rarest category of harm, and it makes children hide ordinary trouble because they expect an alarmed reaction.

Verification works better. Who is this, how would I know, what are they asking me to do, and does it involve hurrying or hiding. Those questions apply to a stranger and to somebody they know, which matters, because statistically the second is far more likely to be the problem.

Misconceptions to address

  • That danger looks like a stranger. Most harm to children online comes from people they already know, and the stranger-danger frame reliably points attention the wrong way.
  • That a profile with photographs and friends is proof of anything. All of it is cheap to fabricate and always has been.
  • That scams target the naive. Good ones work by creating urgency, which defeats careful people just as well.
  • Adults are the most frequently scammed age group, which is worth admitting out loud.

Running this with a class

Show three message screenshots, one genuine and two manipulative, and have the class identify the pressure technique in each: urgency, secrecy, flattery, authority. Naming the technique generalises far better than naming the scam, because the scams change and the techniques do not.
