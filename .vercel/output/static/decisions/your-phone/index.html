What about my own phone?

The short answer

The rules that survive in a house are the ones that apply to the house. Elsewhere on this site you are asked, in passing, to charge your phone in the kitchen with everyone else's and to keep it off the table at meals. This page is why. Your child's model of what a phone is for, and of what adults do with idle minutes, is assembled from watching you long before any rule you set gets a hearing. The good news is that everything this site recommends for kids works on adults, because it was never about age.

  1. Start here

    Your phone charges overnight where everyone's does, and it stays off the table at meals. The rule you set for them, held by you first.

    For families who want the version that costs one habit and buys the credibility for every other rule.

  2. Going further

    While your kids are awake and home, the phone lives in one fixed spot, like a landline. Notifications from people you know stay on. Everything else goes.

    For families who notice they check it between every task and would rather the checking had a location.

  3. All the way

    A phone with nothing that pulls: feeds off or gone, screen in grayscale, apps that exist to be checked deleted. Photographs of your children go up only with their say-so.

    For families who have watched their own attention fray and want the device demoted to a tool.

The example is most of the teaching

Long before a child can hold an argument about screens, they have absorbed a model of what the object is for. Where it sits at dinner. Whether it wins when it buzzes mid-sentence. What a parent’s face does in a quiet moment: settles, or drops to a glow.

Every script on this site leans on this already. The bedroom rule works when “mine is out here too” is true. The hard-to-put-down conversation lands when you can say “right up until I do” about yourself without wincing. You cannot teach why it is hard to put down while visibly unable to put yours down, and children notice the gap between rule and practice years before they can name it.

What the evidence shows

Researchers call it technoference: technology interrupting time between parent and child. In a study of 170 American families with children averaging three years old, parents who rated their own phone use as a problem reported more of those interruptions, and more interruptions tracked with more behaviour problems in the child.

Hold it as lightly as this site holds every other correlation. Parents reported on themselves, and the arrow can run both ways, because a child having a hard week also sends a parent to the phone. What the study establishes is smaller and still useful: the parent’s phone belongs in any honest conversation about the child’s.

This is not a virtue problem

The same design that makes the feed hard for a twelve-year-old to leave is working on you, tuned by the same measurements. Noticing that you check your phone forty times an evening is not a discovery about your character.

Which is why the advice here is environmental rather than moral, exactly as it is for kids. A phone with a fixed spot gets visited instead of carried. A grayscale screen is a duller slot machine. Notifications cut to actual humans turn the phone back into a phone. None of this requires willpower after the first week, and the first week is annoying, and that is the whole cost.

The photographs

One more habit belongs on this page because your child cannot set it for you.

Adults post their children publicly for years, then ask those same children to be careful about what they share online. The correction is cheap: ask first, and accept no. A four-year-old can hold this exchange, and being asked is how they learn to ask.

What to say

When your kid catches you at the table:

“You’re right. Same rule, my mistake. It’s going in the hall.”

No speech, no justification, and do not soften it with a laugh. Being caught and folding instantly is the most instructive thing they will see a phone do all week.

When you surface from twenty minutes of scrolling in front of them:

“I’ve just lost twenty minutes and I couldn’t tell you to what. I’m putting it away.”

Narrating the exit teaches the exit. Getting pulled under never looks like much from the outside; showing a child what noticing looks like is rarer and more useful than never slipping.

When work needs you reachable:

“I’m on call tonight, so my phone is out and I’ll be checking it. That’s the exception, and you can hold me to the difference.”

When this doesn’t apply

Parents whose phone is the job: on call, running a business from it, the only route to a co-parent or an ageing relative. The advice compresses instead of vanishing. Notifications from the people who matter stay on and the rest go, and narrate what the phone is doing when you pick it up, because “I’m answering Grandma” and silent scrolling look identical from across the room and teach opposite things.

A single parent coordinating an entire household through one device is not failing this page. The fixed spot still helps, if only because it means the coordination happens standing in the hall rather than woven through every minute of the evening.
