Decision 1 of 11

When should our kid get a phone?

The short answer

Later than feels comfortable, and later than your kid will accept without protest. Forget how old she is for a moment and ask what problem you are actually solving. Almost every first phone is bought to solve logistics: knowing where a kid is, reaching her after practice, letting her call home. A smartphone solves that problem at enormous cost. A watch or a call-only handset solves the same problem at almost none. Wait until the problem you have requires a phone, then buy the least capable device that solves it.

  1. Start here

    Separate the logistics from the smartphone. Get a watch or a call-only device for contact, and keep the internet out of your kid's pocket for now.

    For families who need to reach their kid today and are tired of being the only holdout.

  2. Going further

    Hold the line to the end of middle school, and say so out loud to the other parents in your kid's year. The isolation argument collapses the moment three families agree.

    For families who have some room to organise and would rather change the norm than fight it.

  3. All the way

    No personally owned internet-connected device until high school. Shared family devices live in shared rooms, and contact runs through a watch or a landline.

    For families who are ready to be unusual, and would rather absorb the social cost early than unwind a habit later.

Ask a better question

“What age should she get a phone” has no answer, which is why every article about it disappoints you. Maturity is not a birthday. The question that does have an answer is this one: what specific problem are we trying to solve, and what is the smallest device that solves it?

Write down the actual problem. Most families find it is one of three things. She walks home and you want to know she arrived. Practice ends at unpredictable times and she needs to call. Her friends organise their social lives somewhere and she is missing it.

The first two are logistics, and logistics have cheap answers. The third is real and deserves a real response, but it is not solved by handing over a device with an open internet connection and no plan.

Why we lean late

Time is finite. This is the part of the argument that does not depend on any contested research: an hour on a phone is an hour not spent doing something else, and at this age the something else matters a great deal. Unstructured play in early childhood predicts stronger self-regulation years later, a capacity that tracks with health, school and financial outcomes across a lifetime.

Sleep is the clearest single finding in this whole field. When researchers looked at what actually drives the link between screens and sleep in children, it was portable devices, the ones that follow a kid into the bedroom and into the bed. That is a specific, fixable problem, and it is the reason the charger belongs in the kitchen no matter what you decide about the phone itself.

Reading is the other place the evidence is unusually clear. Across dozens of studies, children understood and remembered more from paper than from the same text on a screen.

We will be straight with you about the part that is not settled. Whether smartphones and social media are driving the rise in teenage anxiety and depression is disputed among serious researchers, and the honest summary is that nobody has proved it either way. You do not need that argument to justify waiting. The case for a later phone rests on what a childhood is made of, and it stands whether or not the causal fight ever settles.

What this looks like in daily life

The device that solves logistics is a watch or a call-only handset, and the whole setup takes an afternoon.

Contact list is approved contacts only. The device charges in a shared room overnight, and so does yours, because a rule that applies to one person in the house is a rule that will be argued about every night. If there is a smartphone in the house at all, it belongs to the family and to no child in particular, it lives in a shared room, and it gets used with someone else present.

Say the plan out loud before you need it. “You will get a phone. Not this year.” is a completely different conversation from an argument that starts at the moment of refusal.

What to say

When she says everyone has one:

“Some of them do. I know that is hard, and I am not pretending it isn’t. Here is what I am worried about, and here is what we are doing instead.”

When she says you don’t trust her:

“I trust you. I don’t trust an app built by a company that makes money when you can’t put it down. Those are different things, and the second one isn’t about you.”

When you have already said yes and want to walk it back:

“I got this wrong. I gave you something before either of us had a plan for it, and the fixing is mine to do. You did nothing wrong.”

That last one costs something to say and it works better than pretending the first decision was correct.

When this doesn’t apply

Some families need a phone earlier and should not feel bad about it. Split custody where a kid needs to reach the other parent privately. A medical condition that needs monitoring. A long solo commute. A disabled kid for whom a phone is an access tool rather than a toy. A caregiving situation where the child is the one being relied on.

If that is you, the advice does not change so much as compress: buy the least capable device that does the job, keep it out of the bedroom at night, and start the conversations earlier rather than later.
