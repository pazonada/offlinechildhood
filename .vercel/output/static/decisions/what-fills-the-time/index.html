Decision 7 of 7

What do they actually do instead?

The short answer

Nothing, quite often, and that is the point. The instinct after removing a screen is to fill the gap with something equally structured, which is how families end up with a calendar so full that everyone reaches for a phone to recover from it. What childhood actually needs is unstructured time, outdoors where possible, with other children, and without an adult running it. That is harder to arrange than a screen and harder than a class, which is exactly why it has quietly disappeared.

Three ways to do this

These all point the same direction. They are rungs on a ladder rather than options on a menu, so start wherever you can actually hold the line. A rule you keep beats a rule you announce.

  1. 1Start here

    Protect one genuinely unstructured stretch a week. No plan, no class, no screen. Boredom allowed and expected.

    For families who have a full calendar and want one reliable gap in it.

  2. 2Going further

    Make it outdoors and with other kids, most days, in whatever weather. Buy the coat rather than checking the forecast.

    For families who can shift the shape of an ordinary afternoon.

  3. 3All the way

    Cut organised activities back to what your child genuinely loves, let them roam as far as your area honestly allows, and accept the scraped knees.

    For families who are willing to be the household other parents find slightly odd.

Boredom is not the enemy

The first twenty minutes after a screen goes off are unpleasant. Your child will be at a loose end and will tell you so, repeatedly, and the temptation to solve it is enormous.

Do not solve it. That flat, itchy, nothing-to-do feeling is the raw material for everything that comes next. A child who is never bored never has to invent anything, because something is always being provided. The invention only starts once the provision stops, and it takes longer than you would like.

This is also why swapping a screen for a packed schedule does not work. Both fill the time. Only one of them leaves room for the child to do the filling.

What the evidence actually supports

We are careful elsewhere on this site about overclaiming, so it is worth saying that this is the part where the research is strongest, and it is positive rather than negative.

Time in unstructured free play in the early years predicted stronger self-regulation years later, in a large Australian study following children over time. Self-regulation is not a small outcome. It tracks with health, education and financial outcomes across a lifetime.

Outdoor play that involves some real risk, climbing higher than is entirely comfortable, going a bit further than usual, has a formal position statement from the Canadian Paediatric Society behind it. Their conclusion is that it supports physical activity, social competence, risk perception and self-esteem, and that over-restricting it carries its own costs. Keeping children safe from everything is not a neutral choice.

Notice that none of this is an argument about screens. It is an argument about what the time is for.

What this looks like on a Tuesday

Send them outside without a plan. Not to a park with equipment and a scheduled end, just outside. This feels like neglect the first few times and it is not.

Buy the waterproofs. British and Pacific Northwest parents already know this: the weather stops being a factor the moment the clothing is right, and a huge fraction of the outdoor time other families lose is lost to not owning a coat.

Make it social. One other child transforms an empty afternoon, and it is far easier to arrange than a class.

Leave the ingredients lying around. Cardboard, tape, string, a torch, a magnifying glass, cheap tools, a camera with actual film in it. Objects that do one thing badly invite more invention than objects that do everything well.

Have somewhere to put the results. A shelf, a wall, a windowsill. Making things matters more when the things persist.

What to say

When they say they are bored:

“Good. That’s allowed.”

Then go back to what you were doing. This is much harder than it sounds and it is the whole technique.

When you are tempted to sign them up for another activity:

Ask yourself whether they asked for it. If the honest answer is that it fills a gap you found uncomfortable, leave the gap.

When this doesn’t apply

Roaming freely depends enormously on where you live. A busy road with no crossing, or a genuinely unsafe area, is not a mindset problem to be overcome. The principle still holds, but it might mean a garden, a yard, a courtyard or a regular trip somewhere rather than the front door.

Some children, particularly those who are autistic or have sensory differences, do not experience unstructured time as freeing at all. It can be genuinely distressing. Structure is not the enemy there. Adjust the shape of this: less open-ended, more predictable, still not a screen.
