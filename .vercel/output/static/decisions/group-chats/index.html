Decision 6 of 7

What about group chats and social apps?

The short answer

Hold off on social apps for as long as you can, and take the social cost seriously rather than pretending it is imaginary. This is the decision where holding the line genuinely hurts, because the thing your child loses is real: the group chat is where plans get made and jokes happen, and being outside it is not nothing. The honest answer is not that it does not matter. It is that the answer is other parents, not an app. Two or three families agreeing changes this overnight in a way that no rule in your house can.

Three ways to do this

These all point the same direction. They are rungs on a ladder rather than options on a menu, so start wherever you can actually hold the line. A rule you keep beats a rule you announce.

  1. 1Start here

    No social apps yet, but solve the underlying need. A way to message a short list of actual friends, on a shared or basic device.

    For families who want to hold the line without leaving their kid genuinely stranded.

  2. 2Going further

    Talk to the parents of your kid's three closest friends and agree a shared line. Say the year out loud so it is a plan rather than a refusal.

    For families who can organise a little and would rather change the norm than fight it alone.

  3. 3All the way

    No social platforms through middle school, agreed with a group of families in your kid's year, in writing, at the start of the school year.

    For families who are ready to organise properly, which is the only thing that reliably works.

Why this one is different

Every other decision on this site costs you an argument. This one costs your child something, and it is worth being honest about that rather than talking around it.

When you say no to the group chat, your kid does miss things. Plans genuinely get made there. Jokes happen there and get referenced the next day. The social world of a twelve-year-old is being coordinated somewhere they are not. Telling them that is not real, or that the friendships are not real, does not work, mostly because it is not true, and they know it is not true.

So the honest position is that this decision has a price. What follows is about lowering that price rather than pretending it does not exist.

The price is set by other parents

Here is the thing that changes the maths. Almost none of the cost comes from being without an app. It comes from being the only one without it.

That means the decision is not really a rule you set at home. It is a norm, and norms are set by groups. Three families in a school year deciding together is worth more than any amount of firmness in one household, and it converts your kid’s position from “the only one whose mum said no” to “one of the ones who isn’t on it yet”. Those are entirely different social facts.

Nothing else on this site makes as much difference for as little effort, and nothing else gets skipped as often, because sending that first message to other parents feels awkward. Send it anyway. A surprising number of them are waiting for someone else to go first. Wait Until 8th exists to make exactly this easier, and is worth borrowing from even if you just do it informally over a WhatsApp thread.

Solve the real need separately

Underneath “I want Snapchat” is usually something reasonable: I want to talk to my friends without an adult arranging it.

That is a fair thing to want and it does not require a social platform. A basic phone or a watch with a short approved contact list handles it. So does a shared family device with messaging on it. What you are declining is the feed, the public metrics, the disappearing content and the open door to strangers, and you can decline all of that while still saying yes to talking to friends.

What to say

When they say they are missing everything:

“You are missing some of it. I’m not going to pretend you’re not. Here’s what I’m going to do about it.”

Then actually do something: arrange the in-person thing, get them a way to message the three people who matter, call the other parents.

When they ask when:

“Year nine. I’m telling you now so it’s a plan and not a fight.”

A named year is far better than an indefinite no. It gives them something to hold and it stops every request being a fresh negotiation.

When another parent says yes:

“That’s their call and I’m not judging it. Ours is different.”

When this doesn’t apply

Older teenagers already inside these platforms are a different problem, and removal at that stage usually costs more than it gains. The work there is about settings, sleep and honest conversation rather than prohibition.

A kid whose entire social world is geographically distant, because of a move, or family abroad, or a small rural community, has a genuinely different calculation. The connection may be worth more than the cost. Choose the most boring tool that does the job, and start the conversations earlier than you otherwise would.

Talk about it too

Rules do half the work. These are the conversations that do the other half, written for every age from four up.

  • Who is actually on the other endOnline, you cannot tell who you are talking to, and increasingly you cannot tell whether it is a person at all.
  • What they know about youUsing something free means giving away information about yourself, and that information lasts far longer and travels much further than anyone expects.
  • Is that real?A photograph is no longer evidence that something happened. Working out what is real is now a skill rather than something you can see.
