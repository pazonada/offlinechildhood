Decision 6 of 11

What about group chats and social apps?

The short answer

Hold off on social apps for as long as you can, and take the social cost seriously, because pretending it is imaginary fools no twelve-year-old. This is the decision where holding the line hurts, because the thing your child loses is real: the group chat is where plans get made and jokes happen, and being outside it is not nothing. The honest remedy is other parents. Two or three families agreeing changes this overnight in a way that no rule in your house can.

  1. Start here

    No social apps yet, but solve the underlying need. A way to message a short list of actual friends, on a shared or basic device.

    For families who want to hold the line without leaving their kid stranded.

  2. Going further

    Talk to the parents of your kid's three closest friends and agree a shared line. Say the year out loud so it is a plan rather than a refusal.

    For families who can organise a little and would rather change the norm than fight it alone.

  3. All the way

    No social platforms through middle school, agreed with a group of families in your kid's year, in writing, at the start of the school year.

    For families who are ready to organise properly, which is the only thing that reliably works.

Why this one is different

Every other decision on this site costs you an argument. This one costs your child something, and it is worth being honest about that instead of talking around it.

When you say no to the group chat, your kid does miss things. Plans get made there. Jokes happen there and get referenced the next day. The social world of a twelve-year-old is being coordinated somewhere they are not. Telling them that is not real, or that the friendships are not real, does not work, mostly because it is not true, and they know it is not true.

So the honest position is that this decision has a price. What follows is about lowering that price, since pretending it away does not work.

There is a version of this worth knowing about from the other end of education. When MIT studied how its own students use AI, one of the findings that surprised the committee was social: leaning on a chatbot displaced the study group, the classmate and the office hour. The same substitution is available to a twelve-year-old, and the thing being substituted for is the same thing.

The price is set by other parents

Almost none of the cost comes from being without an app. It comes from being the only one without it.

That makes this less a rule you set at home than a norm, and norms are set by groups. Three families in a school year deciding together is worth more than any amount of firmness in one household, and it converts your kid’s position from “the only one whose mum said no” to “one of the ones who isn’t on it yet”. Those are entirely different social facts.

Nothing else on this site makes as much difference for as little effort, and nothing else gets skipped as often, because sending that first message to other parents feels awkward. Send it anyway. A surprising number of them are waiting for someone else to go first. Wait Until 8th exists to make exactly this easier, and is worth borrowing from even if you just do it informally over a WhatsApp thread.

Solve the real need separately

Underneath “I want Snapchat” is usually something reasonable: I want to talk to my friends without an adult arranging it.

That is a fair thing to want and it does not require a social platform. A basic phone or a watch with a short approved contact list handles it. So does a shared family device with messaging on it. What you are declining is the feed, the public metrics, the disappearing content and the open door to strangers, and you can decline all of that while still saying yes to talking to friends.

What to say

When they say they are missing everything:

“You are missing some of it. I’m not going to pretend you’re not. Here’s what I’m going to do about it.”

Then actually do something: arrange the in-person thing, get them a way to message the three people who matter, call the other parents.

When they ask when:

“Year nine. I’m telling you now so it’s a plan and not a fight.”

A named year is far better than an indefinite no. It gives them something to hold and it stops every request being a fresh negotiation.

When another parent says yes:

“That’s their call and I’m not judging it. Ours is different.”

When this doesn’t apply

Older teenagers already inside these platforms are a different problem, and removal at that stage usually costs more than it gains. The work there is about settings, sleep and honest conversation; prohibition at that stage costs more than it gets.

A kid whose entire social world is geographically distant, because of a move, or family abroad, or a small rural community, has a different calculation. The connection may be worth more than the cost. Choose the most boring tool that does the job, and start the conversations earlier than you otherwise would.
