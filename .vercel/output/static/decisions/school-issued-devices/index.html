Decision 9 of 10

What do I do about the laptop my kid's school handed them?

The short answer

You almost certainly cannot refuse it, so the decision is not whether but how it lives in your house. Treat it as a work tool that visits: it does homework in a shared room and then goes back on a shelf, and it never sleeps in a bedroom. Then do the one thing almost no parent does, which is ask the school what is switched on inside it. The answer changed this August, and it changed without teachers being asked.

Three ways to do this

These all point the same direction. They are rungs on a ladder rather than options on a menu, so start wherever you can actually hold the line. A rule you keep beats a rule you announce.

  1. 1Start here

    The school device charges downstairs with everything else and does not go into a bedroom. Homework happens where you can see it.

    For families who want the single change that costs nothing and needs no permission from the school.

  2. 2Going further

    Email the school and ask three things. What AI tools are switched on, who can see what my child types into them, and can I opt out. Ask in writing.

    For families who are willing to have one polite exchange with an administrator to find out what is actually running.

  3. 3All the way

    Ask for paper where paper does the job better, especially reading. Then take the defaults question to the people who set them, at a board or governors' meeting.

    For families who think the choice of what runs on a child's school day should involve the families whose children use it.

The thing that changed in August

On 10 August 2026, Google began rolling out Gemini in Classroom to students of all ages. The sentence that matters is Google’s own: access to the Gemini tab in Classroom is on by default for teachers and students of all ages.

On by default. Not offered, not opted into, not announced to you.

Access is controlled by whoever administers your school’s Google account. There is no teacher-level switch, so the teacher standing in front of your child may have had no say in it and may not know it happened. There is no parental notification in the announcement at all. If a school wants it off for under eighteens, an administrator has to build a separate organisational unit and turn it off there.

So a reasonable thing to assume is that your child has a conversational AI inside their school account right now, and that nobody told either of you.

What to actually ask

Three questions, in an email, to whoever runs IT rather than to the class teacher, who probably cannot answer them:

Which AI tools are enabled on my child’s account. Who can see what my child types into them. And is there a way to opt my child out.

Ask in writing. Not to be difficult, but because the answer tends to be more precise when somebody has to type it, and because you will want to know what you were told when it changes again.

When the answer comes back, pass it on to your child rather than filing it. A rule somebody else set is the hardest kind to keep, and it holds far better when the person living under it knows what it is for, which is the argument made at more length under AI and schoolwork.

Where the evidence actually points, including against me

It is tempting to say these machines are bad for learning. The research does not support that, and this site is not going to pretend otherwise.

The best pooled evidence on giving every child a laptop found small positive effects on achievement in English, writing, maths and science, in the region of 0.16 standard deviations. Small, and much smaller than things like tutoring, but positive. Only ten studies were solid enough to pool, so it is a thin base. It is still the opposite of the claim that the devices are making children worse at school.

The sharper finding is about what the device replaces. Across 54 studies and more than 170,000 readers, comprehension was reliably better on paper than on screen, and the gap grew over the years rather than closing as screens improved. It is worst for informational text and worst when the reader is working against the clock, which describes most reading a school sets.

So the honest version is narrower than the complaint and more useful than it. The laptop is roughly fine as a laptop. It is a poor substitute for a book, and a school that has quietly moved its reading onto screens has traded away something measurable for something convenient.

That is a question you can ask, and it lands better than a general objection to technology, because it is specific and there is evidence behind it.

The part that is about the company, not the child

Chromebooks are in schools at the scale they are because they are cheap to buy and cheap to administer, which are procurement virtues rather than educational ones. It is worth being clear-eyed that this is a purchasing decision that happened to become a pedagogical one.

It has consequences beyond the classroom. Denmark’s data protection regulator examined how student data reached Google through these devices, concluded there was no legal basis for all of the purposes involved, and ordered 53 municipalities to change what they were doing. That is a regulator, not a campaign group, and it is about data rather than about learning. Both things can be true: the device is adequate for schoolwork, and the arrangement around it deserves more scrutiny than a purchase order gets.

What is still yours

Not much of the device. Quite a lot of the surroundings.

Where it charges is yours. Whether it goes into a bedroom is yours. Whether your child understands what the assistant inside it actually is, and why it agrees with them so readily, is entirely yours, and is covered in why it acts like it likes you.

And the reading is more yours than it feels. A child who does the set reading on paper is not opting out of school. They are taking the version the evidence prefers.

Talk about it too

Rules do half the work. These are the conversations that do the other half, written for every age from four up.

  • What they know about youUsing something free means giving away information about yourself, and that information lasts far longer and travels much further than anyone expects.
  • Doing the hard part yourselfThe struggle is not in the way of the learning. The struggle is the learning, and handing it to a machine skips the part that was doing the work.
  • Why it acts like it likes youA chatbot that seems to care about you was built to seem that way, because people who feel cared about keep typing.

Where this comes from

  1. Google Workspace Updates, Google, 2026

    Google's own announcement. It states that access to the Gemini tab in Classroom is on by default for teachers and students of all ages, rolling out from 10 August 2026 on the web. Access is controlled by the school's Workspace administrator. The post describes no teacher-level control and no parental notification.

  2. Zheng, B., Warschauer, M., Lin, C.-H., & Chang, C., Review of Educational Research, 2016

    Pooling the usable studies, one-to-one laptop programmes produced small positive effects on achievement in English, writing, mathematics and science, around 0.16 standard deviations. This is evidence against the claim that giving every child a laptop harms achievement. It is a small effect next to interventions like tutoring, and only ten studies met the bar for the statistical pooling, so the base is thin.

  3. Delgado, P., Vargas, C., Ackerman, R., & Salmerón, L., Educational Research Review, 2018

    Across 54 studies and more than 170,000 readers, comprehension was better on paper than on screen, and the advantage for paper grew over the years rather than shrinking as screens improved. Strongest where the reader is working against a time limit and for informational text.

  4. Datatilsynet, the Danish Data Protection Authority, Datatilsynet, 2024

    A national regulator found that the way student data reached Google through Chromebooks and Google Workspace lacked a legal basis for all of the purposes it was disclosed for, and ordered 53 municipalities to change their practices. It is a data protection finding and says nothing about whether the devices help children learn.

  5. Common Sense Media Youth AI Safety Institute, Common Sense Media, 2026

    A survey of 1,204 children aged 9 to 17. 86 per cent use AI and nearly a quarter use it daily, but more than four in ten say no parent or guardian has ever talked to them about AI safety. Only a third know AI cannot reliably tell fact from fiction. Over half of users have asked it about their health or body and more than a third have used it to discuss feelings; a quarter of those say it sometimes understands them better than most people. One in six have seen inappropriate material through a chatbot and only a third told an adult. Kids who find it hard to focus on schoolwork lean on AI more.
