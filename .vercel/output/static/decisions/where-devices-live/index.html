Decision 2 of 11

Where should screens live in the house?

The short answer

Out of bedrooms, and charging somewhere shared overnight. If you do one thing from this entire site, do this one. It costs nothing, it takes an afternoon, and unlike most of what gets argued about here, the evidence behind it is not seriously contested. Screens that follow a kid to bed are the ones that cost them sleep. Everything else you are worried about gets harder when a child is tired.

  1. Start here

    One shared charging spot in the kitchen or hallway. Everything plugs in there at night, including your phone.

    For families who want a single change that works from tonight and does not need anyone's agreement.

  2. Going further

    No screens in bedrooms at any hour, and no devices at the table. Bedrooms are for sleeping and reading.

    For families who have the room to make it a house rule rather than a bedtime rule.

  3. All the way

    Every screen lives in one shared room and gets used with somebody else present. Bedrooms have a book and an alarm clock.

    For families who are setting the pattern early and would rather never unwind it later.

Why this one first

Most of what parents argue about here is uncertain. This part is not.

When researchers looked at what actually drives the relationship between screens and children’s sleep, the answer was portable devices specifically, the ones a kid can carry into a bedroom and into the bed itself. In a population sample of American children from birth to seventeen, that was where the effect lived.

Think about what a phone in a bedroom actually is. It is a device engineered by very capable people to be difficult to put down, handed to a tired child, alone, in the dark, with nobody watching and no natural stopping point. Of course it wins. It would win against us.

And sleep is upstream of everything else on your list. Attention, mood, patience, appetite, the ability to have a reasonable conversation about screen time at all. A tired child is worse at every single thing you are trying to help them with.

What this looks like in daily life

Buy a cheap multi-port charger. Put it in the kitchen or the hallway, somewhere public and slightly inconvenient. Everything charges there overnight.

Buy each kid an actual alarm clock, about ten pounds or dollars. This removes the only reasonable objection anyone will raise, and it removes it for the price of a takeaway.

Your phone goes on the charger too. This is the part people skip, and it is the part that makes the whole thing work. A rule that applies to the children and not to the adults is a rule your children will spend the next decade litigating, and they will be right to.

If a kid needs audio to fall asleep, that is fair, and it does not require a screen. A Yoto or a Toniebox plays stories and music with no display, no feed and no way to end up somewhere else at eleven at night.

What to say

When they ask why:

“Because everyone in this house sleeps better without a screen in the room, me included. That’s why mine is out here too.”

When they say they only use it as an alarm:

“Fair. Here’s an alarm clock.”

When they say they will not look at it:

“I believe you mean that. I also don’t look at mine, right up until I do. It’s not a willpower thing. The thing is built to be hard to put down.”

When you are the one breaking the rule:

“You’re right, I had my phone upstairs last night. Same rule; I broke it.”

When this doesn’t apply

A kid who needs a device for a medical alert, or to reach a parent they do not live with, or as an accessibility tool. In those cases the device stays, and the rest still applies: it charges across the room rather than under the pillow, and notifications go off at night.

Teenagers who have had a phone in the bedroom for years are a harder case, and pulling it out overnight will feel to them like a punishment for something they have not done. Start by making the change together with a reason attached instead of announcing it. If that fails, negotiate the charger being across the room before you negotiate it leaving. Distance is most of the benefit.

Talk about it too

Rules do half the work. These are the conversations that do the other half, written for every age from four up.
