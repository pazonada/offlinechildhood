How do I keep my kid safe in games that have other players in them?

The short answer

Separate the game from the channel, because they arrive bundled and almost every parent approves both while thinking they approved one. Your child can build in Minecraft or play Roblox with open chat switched off and a friends list made of people they know in life. Two settings do most of the work, and both take about five minutes. Turn chat down to friends only. Then block transfers of in-game currency from adults, which is a safeguarding control rather than a spending one, for reasons worth understanding.

Three ways to do this

These all point the same direction. They are rungs on a ladder rather than options on a menu, so start wherever you can actually hold the line. A rule you keep beats a rule you announce.

  1. 1Start here

    Chat set to friends only, the friends list made of people your child knows offline, and in-game currency transfers from adults blocked.

    For families who want the two settings that matter tonight, without ending anything.

  2. 2Going further

    Games with other people in them happen in a shared room. You go through the friends list together every so often and ask who each person actually is.

    For families who are happy for their child to play socially, as long as it is not happening out of sight.

  3. 3All the way

    Private or invited servers only, no open matchmaking, and voice chat only with named friends whose parents you could ring.

    For families who would rather their child's online play be an extension of friendships that already exist.

Start by not making the game the enemy

Before any of the rest of it: for one in three young people in a survey of 1,200 nine to seventeen year olds, their closest friendship had formed online.

Read that before the warnings, because it is why a blanket ban tends to fail and to cost more than it saves. A child building something with three friends on a server is doing something sociable and often genuinely creative, and treating it as a threat teaches them that you do not understand the thing and cannot be consulted about it. You want to be consultable. Everything below depends on it.

The channel is the risk, not the game

Minecraft and Roblox arrive as two things wearing one name. There is a world your child plays in, and there is an open communication channel to whoever else is present. Parents approve the first and receive the second, usually without ever making a decision about it.

You can separate them. Chat can be limited to friends, voice can be off, and the game underneath carries on being the game. The single most useful sentence here is that allowing a game is not the same as allowing an open channel to adults you have never met, and the two have entirely different settings.

Roblox is not one game so much as a platform of worlds made by other users, and a world somebody uploaded last week is a different proposition from something a studio built and tested. Judge the room, not the brand on the door.

The move to watch for

In the same survey, two thirds of children said somebody they had met online had asked them to move from a public place into a private conversation on a different app.

That is the step worth teaching, because it is the point where the conversation leaves anywhere you can see. It is also gloriously easy to describe. Somebody asking you to carry on this chat somewhere else is the signal, whoever they are, however nice they have been, and no matter what reason they give. Not a reason to panic. A reason to come and say so.

Teach the move rather than a list of dangerous apps. The apps change every year and the move has not changed at all.

Gifts are not generosity

This is the part that surprises people, and it is where your worries about payments and about strangers turn out to be one worry.

Buying things for a child creates obligation and, more usefully to an adult with bad intentions, creates something to keep quiet about. In games this looks like Robux, or a skin, or a battle pass appearing as a present from somebody generous.

You do not have to take my word for the significance. In April 2026 Roblox settled with the Attorney General of Alabama, and among the changes it agreed to make was giving parents the ability to restrict transfers of in-game currency from adults to their children. A state regulator wrote that control into a legal settlement, which tells you what it is for.

So when this site tells you elsewhere to switch off in-game purchases because they are the engine behind compulsive design, that is true and it is only half the reason. The same setting closes a door.

What is different this year

The same settlement requires Roblox to check the age of all users, and to stop adults and under sixteens from chatting at all unless they are identified as trusted friends. Communications involving children are not to be encrypted, so that police can act on them.

A settlement is not a court finding that a company harmed anyone, and it should not be read as one. What it means practically is that controls which did not exist a year ago exist now. Go and look at the parental settings on any account your child already has, because they will not have been switched on for you.

They will not necessarily walk away

Here is the finding that should change how you plan.

Nearly a quarter of the children surveyed stayed in contact with somebody who had made them uncomfortable.

Most parents are relying, without realising it, on a plan that goes: something feels wrong, so my child blocks them and tells me. That is not what tends to happen. Discomfort and continued contact sit together quite happily, especially where a friendship has been built first, which is the entire purpose of building one.

Which means the settings matter more than they ought to, and the standing promise matters most of all. Say it before it is needed: if anything goes wrong in there, telling me makes it better and not worse, nobody is losing the game over it, and I will not be angry. Then, when something small happens, prove it.

The techniques these conversations run on are the same ones covered under who is actually on the other end, and are worth naming with your child directly.

What to say

When you want to see who they play with:

“Who’s on your friends list? Tell me about them. Which ones do you know from actual life?”

When you set the currency control:

“This one isn’t about money. If a grown-up ever wants to buy you something in a game, that’s the bit I want to know about.”

When you teach the move:

“If anyone ever asks you to talk somewhere else, another app, a private chat, anything, that’s the moment you come and tell me. You won’t be in trouble. That’s the whole rule.”

When this doesn’t apply

Single-player and local co-op games raise none of this. Neither does a private server with four children who know each other from school, which is most of what Minecraft actually is in practice.

And a teenager with a long-standing online friendship is not in the same situation as a nine-year-old in an open lobby. The older the child, the more this becomes a conversation about how manipulation works rather than a set of switches, because the switches stop being enforceable and the understanding is what has to carry them.

Talk about it too

Rules do half the work. These are the conversations that do the other half, written for every age from four up.

  • Who is actually on the other endOnline, you cannot tell who you are talking to, and increasingly you cannot tell whether it is a person at all.
  • Who pays for freeIf you are not paying for something, someone else is, and they are paying for your attention. That single fact explains most of how these products behave.

Where this comes from

  1. Thorn, Thorn, 2022

    A survey of 1,200 young people aged 9 to 17. Two thirds said somebody they met online had asked them to move from a public forum into a private conversation on a different platform. Nearly a quarter stayed in contact with somebody who had made them uncomfortable, and for one in three their closest friendship had formed online. Survey research by a child-safety organisation rather than a peer-reviewed study, and the authors state plainly that stigma around the subject probably means undercounting. The figures cover online life in general and are not specific to games.

  2. Office of the Attorney General of Alabama, Alabama Attorney General, 2026

    A settlement announced on 21 April 2026 requiring Roblox to verify the age of all users, to stop adults and under sixteens chatting unless they are identified as trusted friends, to let parents restrict transfers of in-game currency from adults, and to leave communications involving minors unencrypted for law enforcement. A settlement is not a finding of wrongdoing. What it establishes for a parent is that these controls now exist and can be switched on.
