Decision 8 of 11

What do we do about AI and homework?

The short answer

Ban it and you lose, allow it freely and they lose. The question that works is not whether your kid uses AI for schoolwork but whether it is doing the hard part or making a harder part possible. Explaining a concept a fourth way, checking finished work, arguing back against a draft: all fine, sometimes better than fine. Producing the answer they were supposed to produce: not fine, and the real cost has nothing to do with school rules. The struggle they skipped was the thing that would have taught them.

  1. Start here

    One rule, agreed together. Attempt it first, and be able to explain the finished work to someone without looking.

    For families who want something enforceable tonight that does not require policing every device.

  2. Going further

    Homework happens in a shared room. Agree per subject where AI helps and where it defeats the point, and ask the school what its actual policy is.

    For families who have the time to make it a running conversation rather than a rule.

  3. All the way

    No AI for schoolwork through primary. Paper, books, a person to ask. Introduce the tools deliberately later, as a subject in their own right.

    For families who would rather build the foundations first and teach the tools as tools afterwards.

Why the obvious rules both fail

Forbidding it does not work, and not only because it is unenforceable. The tools are useful, your child will use them for the rest of their life, and a rule that treats them as contraband teaches nothing except to hide the tab.

Permitting it freely fails differently. Nobody gets caught. Homework gets done faster, the work looks fine, everyone is pleased, and the learning simply does not happen. There is no moment where this becomes visible, which is exactly what makes it worth attending to.

So draw the line around the task. Rules about the tool are the ones that fail.

The question that actually works

Is it doing the hard part, or making a harder part possible?

Reasonable, and often better than working alone: having a concept explained a fourth way when the textbook and the teacher have both failed. Checking finished work and asking why something is wrong. Arguing with a draft. Getting unstuck at eleven at night when the alternative is giving up. Attempting something beyond them and understanding what came back.

Not reasonable, however it is framed: producing the thing they were meant to produce. Writing the essay. Solving the problem set. Summarising the book instead of reading it.

That question keeps working on tools that do not exist yet, which no rule about any particular app will.

Say why, or the rule will not hold

Whatever you land on, the reason has to travel with it.

A white paper for university computing departments makes this point better than anything else written this year, and it transfers straight to a kitchen table. A rule that says you may not use AI on this piece of work reads as a prohibition for its own sake. The same rule, stated as this task exists to build something you will need later, connects the restriction to the thing it is protecting. Their argument is that people who understand a limit engage with it honestly, and people who do not simply route around it.

Which is worth taking seriously, because a rule your child treats as arbitrary turns into a detection problem, and you will lose it. Secret use is worse than negotiated use in every respect: you cannot see it, you cannot shape it, and you find out about it late.

So say the reason out loud, every time, even when it feels laboured. Not because your child will agree, but because a reason can be argued with and a decree cannot, and you would much rather have the argument.

What the evidence says

This is now documented at the top of the education system. MIT spent five months studying how its own students use AI and reported that learning requires productive struggle, and that offloading it leaves an illusion of learning in its place: you recognise the answer and could not have produced it.

The report names the trap precisely. Cognitive surrender is falling back on the machine at the very first sign of difficulty, which is exactly the moment the learning was about to happen. Students told the committee the pull was strongest when a deadline was close, which is worth knowing, because it means this is a problem of pressure rather than character.

Two findings from a survey of 1,204 children aged 9 to 17 sharpen this. Kids who find it hard to stay focused on schoolwork lean on AI the most, which means the tool is most attractive exactly where the struggle would have done the most good. And schools are teaching rules while judgement goes untaught: three quarters of kids say their school has said what they may and may not use AI for, but only just over half have been taught how to use it safely. The gap between those two numbers is the space this page is trying to fill.

The early signals the MIT report records are worth stating plainly: diminished critical thinking, weakened memory, eroded confidence, undermined mastery. And a social cost that surprised the committee, which is that leaning on a chatbot displaces the study group, the classmate and the teacher.

Notice who is saying this. MIT builds this technology and the report is openly enthusiastic about what it makes possible. It still concludes that a student who lets it do the thinking has been robbed of the thing they came for.

What this looks like in daily life

Attempt first. Whatever the rule ends up being, the first pass is theirs. The struggle was the homework all along.

Then the explain-it test, which does most of the work on its own: they have to be able to talk through the finished piece without looking at it. A child who used AI well can. A child who let it do the work cannot, and both of you find out in about thirty seconds, without an argument about what happened.

Ask the school what its policy actually is, subject by subject. Most schools have something and many have not told parents. Your kid is likely getting different rules in different classrooms, which is confusing for them and useful for you to know.

Do the homework alongside them sometimes, on your own laptop, on your own work. Nothing teaches this faster than a child watching an adult decide not to take the shortcut.

What to say

When they say everyone uses it:

“Probably. What did you use it for?”

That question is not a trap and should not sound like one. Half the time the answer is completely reasonable and you can say so, which is what buys you the other half.

When you suspect the essay is not theirs:

“Talk me through it. Not to catch you out, I just want to hear it.”

When they say it explained it better than the teacher:

“Good, that’s a real use. Now do the next one without it and see if it stuck.”

When they are up against a deadline and reaching for it:

“You’re not stuck, you’re late. Those are different problems and this only fixes one of them.”

When this doesn’t apply

Assistive use is a different category entirely. For a dyslexic child, or one with a working memory difficulty, or one writing in a second language, these tools can remove a barrier that was never the point of the exercise. The hard part there is the thinking, and the tool is clearing the obstacle in front of it. That is the best possible use.

Older students working at the edge of what they can do alone are also a different case. Using AI to attempt something past that edge, and understanding what comes back, is the thing the MIT report calls augmentation, and it is worth encouraging.

Talk about it too

Rules do half the work. These are the conversations that do the other half, written for every age from four up.

    Common Sense Media Youth AI Safety Institute. The Common Sense Media Census: AI Use by Tweens and Teens (2026). Common Sense Media, 2026.
