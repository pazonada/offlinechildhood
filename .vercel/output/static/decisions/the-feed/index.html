Decision 5 of 8

How do we handle YouTube?

The short answer

Let them watch things. Do not let something else choose what comes next. Almost everything that goes wrong with YouTube and children happens in the gap between one video ending and the next one starting, because that gap is filled by a system optimised for watch time rather than for your kid. Searching for something and watching it is a normal activity. Being fed for ninety minutes is a different one, and the difference is two settings.

Three ways to do this

These all point the same direction. They are rungs on a ladder rather than options on a menu, so start wherever you can actually hold the line. A rule you keep beats a rule you announce.

  1. 1Start here

    Turn off autoplay and turn off notifications. Watch by searching or from a playlist you or they chose deliberately.

    For families who want the single change that removes most of the problem in about two minutes.

  2. 2Going further

    No personal account and no recommendation history. Watching happens on the TV in a shared room rather than on a personal device.

    For families who want to keep video social and visible without banning it.

  3. 3All the way

    No open YouTube in the house. Download or buy specific things to watch, or use a service with no algorithmic feed at all.

    For families who have decided the feed is not worth managing and would rather not have it in the house.

The video is not the problem

Parents tend to worry about a specific video their child might see. That is worth some attention, but it is not where the damage usually is.

The real issue is structural. When a video ends, a system decides what plays next, and that system is measured on how long people keep watching. It is not malicious and there is no person choosing. It is an optimiser doing exactly what it was built to do, and what it discovers, over billions of attempts, is that certain things hold attention better than others. More extreme is stickier than moderate. Louder is stickier than quiet. A thing that makes you slightly anxious is stickier than a thing that leaves you content.

Nobody designed that outcome. It emerged from the target, which is more unsettling than a villain would be, and it is why arguing about individual videos misses the point.

Two settings do most of the work

Autoplay off. This one switch converts watching from something that continues by default into something that requires a decision. It is the difference between a child choosing to watch a fourth video and a child simply not stopping.

Notifications off. There is no version of a YouTube notification that serves your child. Every one of them exists to restart a session that had ended.

That is genuinely most of it. No parental control software, no time limits, no subscription. Two switches.

What this looks like in daily life

Watch on the biggest screen in the house rather than the smallest. A television in the living room is a social object. A phone under a duvet is not. Same content, entirely different behaviour around it.

Use search and playlists rather than the home page. Make a playlist together for things they actually like. It takes ten minutes and it removes the feed from the equation without removing the videos.

Sit through some of it. Not all, and not as supervision. This is the richest possible ground for the conversations, because the mechanism is happening live in front of you and you can just point at it.

Do not rely on the kids’ version of the app to solve this. It is better than nothing, and it is still a recommendation feed with the same incentives.

What to say

When you turn autoplay off:

“I turned off the bit that picks the next one. You can still pick anything you want. I’d just rather you were the one picking.”

When they land somewhere strange:

“How did you end up here? Let’s trace it back.”

Do that without any annoyance in your voice and you get two things: a genuinely interesting archaeology lesson, and a kid who tells you next time.

When they say it is their favourite thing:

“I know. I’m not taking it away. I’m taking away the part that decides for you.”

When this doesn’t apply

Video is a real interest for a lot of kids, and some of them are learning to make it. A child studying editing, or animation, or how a particular creator builds a story, is doing something worth encouraging. The feed is still the feed, but the watching has a point, and treating it as the same activity as idle scrolling will land as unfair because it is.

Households where a screen genuinely buys a parent the twenty minutes needed to cook dinner are not failing at anything. Autoplay off, playlist on, and let it go.

Talk about it too

Rules do half the work. These are the conversations that do the other half, written for every age from four up.

  • Why you're seeing thisNothing on a screen got there by accident. A company chose it, and it chose what keeps you looking.
  • Who pays for freeIf you are not paying for something, someone else is, and they are paying for your attention. That single fact explains most of how these products behave.
  • Designed to be hard to put downThe difficulty you feel stopping was engineered by people who are very good at their jobs. It is not a flaw in you.
