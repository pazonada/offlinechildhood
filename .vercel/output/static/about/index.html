Why Offline Childhood?

We're parents, and we're trying. Trying to get outside more, delay the smartphone, keep dinner screen-free, and raise kids who can think for themselves about all of it.

We are not purists. Nobody here lives offline, and if you do, we salute you. What we think is that the culture has gotten badly out of hand on screen addiction, algorithmic feeds, and now AI, and that childhood is where that costs the most.

So we argue for the analog side. More unstructured play, more paper, more time outdoors, more boredom. Not because technology is evil, but because an hour spent on a screen is an hour spent on something else, and at this age the something else matters enormously.

Why we teach kids how the tech works too

Because they are going to use it. Probably in their careers, certainly for the rest of their lives. A kid who does not understand how an algorithm decides what she sees, or where an AI model got its material, or who is paying for the free app, is not protected. She is just unprepared.

That is what the Conversations half of this site is for. Every idea is written four times over, for ages 4 to 6 through 13 and up, because a four-year-old can absolutely understand that a person chose what she is watching. Limits buy you the years in which understanding gets built. You need both.

What we will not do

Scare you. Fear is the easiest way to get a parent's attention and about the worst way to actually help. There is already plenty of it going around. Your worries here are reasonable, and we are not going to farm them.

Overstate the research. Whether smartphones caused the rise in teenage anxiety is a live fight among serious researchers, and we say so on the pages where it comes up. The case for a more analog childhood does not need that argument, and a site that oversells its evidence deserves to be doubted on everything else.

Tell you that you have already blown it. Most people arrive here with devices in the house and habits already set. Every recommendation on this site has a first step that a family in exactly that position can take this week.

Who is it for?

Parents and caregivers. The advice leans younger, because that is where most of these decisions get made for the first time, but plenty of it works just as well with teenagers, and every conversation is written for older kids too. Teachers as well: they work with one kid at a dinner table or a class of thirty, and each one has a note on running it as a group.

How is it paid for?

At the moment it is not. Nothing on this site is sponsored, no link pays us, and there is no advertising. We may eventually earn a commission on some product links, and if we do, every one of them will be labelled where it appears. The structure is built for that in advance: recommendations only ever show up after a decision has been argued through on its own terms, and the honest answer to most of these questions is that you do not need to buy anything at all.
