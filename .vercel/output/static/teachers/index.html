For teachers

13 lessons you can run this week

Most schools have told students what they may and may not do with AI. Far fewer have taught them how any of it works. In a 2026 survey of 1,204 children, three quarters said their school had set the rules and only just over half had been taught to use these tools safely. The rules are the easy half.

Every activity below is one lesson, and most need no devices at all. Pick the age you teach once, at the top, and the whole page rewrites itself to that age. Then print it. You should not have to open thirteen pages and choose the age on each one.

Which age group are you teaching?

The wording under each activity is written for a parent at the kitchen table, because that is where most of these conversations have to happen. Say it in your own words. What it gives you is the altitude: how much of the machinery a child that age can actually hold.

What it is

What people mean by AI

AI is a machine that has read an enormous amount and guesses what usually comes next. It is not a person, and it does not know things the way you know them.

Play the prediction game as a whole class. Write half a familiar sentence on the board and have everyone call out the ending. The room converges on the same few words, and that convergence is the mechanism. Then write a sentence with no obvious ending and watch the guesses scatter. That is what a model does when it invents something.

Ages 4-6

Keep it to guessing. "It's a computer that's very good at guessing what comes next, because it has seen a huge number of examples. Like how you know that after 'once upon a' comes 'time'." That is the whole idea, and it is genuinely the whole idea.

Listen for. Her saying it guesses. At this age you are not building understanding of machine learning, you are preventing the assumption that it is alive.

Ages 7-9

Bring in the reading. "People fed it an enormous amount of text, more than anyone could read in a lifetime, and it learned which words usually go together. So when you ask it something, it works out what would probably come next, one bit at a time. It is not looking up an answer. It is building one."

Listen for. Her asking how it can be wrong if it read everything. That is exactly the right question and it opens the next concept.

Ages 10-12

Add prediction and probability. "It is a prediction machine. Given everything so far, what word is most likely next? Do that a few hundred times and you get a paragraph. That is why it writes so fluently and why it can be completely wrong with total confidence. Fluency and accuracy are separate things, and it is only optimised for one of them."

Listen for. The distinction between sounding right and being right. If she can say that out loud in her own words, she is ahead of most adults.

Ages 13+

Now the interesting part, which is that nobody fully knows what is happening inside. Researchers can describe the training and the architecture, and they cannot reliably explain why a specific model produced a specific output. This is a live research field, not a gap in your knowledge. It is worth sitting with, because a system that is powerful and not fully explicable is a genuinely new kind of object.

Listen for. Recognition that its explanation of itself is just more prediction. That is the sophisticated version of this concept.

How to explain it at each age

How it works

Why you're seeing this

Nothing on a screen got there by accident. A company chose it, and it chose what keeps you looking.

Have every student open the same app on their own device and search the same word, then compare the first five results across the room. The differences do the teaching for you. For a class without devices, run it as a thought experiment with two invented students whose interests you describe, and have the class predict what each one gets shown.

Ages 4-6

Skip the word algorithm. Try this instead: "Whoever made this show wants you to keep watching, so they made the next one start all by itself. That was their choice, not yours." Keep it to the fact that a person made a choice, because that is the whole idea at this age.

Listen for. Any version of "somebody wanted me to." She does not need the mechanism. She needs to know a person is on the other end of it.

Ages 7-9

Now you can name it. "An algorithm is a set of instructions, like a recipe. People wrote a recipe that decides which video you see next. The recipe's job is to keep you watching, because the company earns money from ads while you watch." Seven-year-olds understand recipes and they understand rules, which is all this requires.

Listen for. "Why did you get different ones than me?" That question means the idea has landed and you can stop explaining.

Ages 10-12

Introduce the money. "These apps are free, so you are not the customer. Advertisers are the customer, and your attention is the product being sold. That is not a conspiracy, it is just the business model, and it explains almost every design choice you notice." Then name the choices: autoplay, infinite scroll, streaks, the pull to refresh, notifications timed for when you are most likely to open them.

Listen for. Her spotting a design choice you did not point out. That is the skill transferring, and it is the point of the whole exercise.

Ages 13+

Engagement optimisation has second-order effects nobody chose. A system tuned for attention will find that outrage travels further than nuance, that extremes hold longer than moderation, and that a body-comparison spiral is very engaging indeed. No engineer sat down and decided this. It emerged from the objective, and that is a more unsettling fact than a villain would be.

Listen for. Recognition that the system has no intent. The sophisticated version of this idea is not that the company is evil, it is that an optimiser does not care.

How to explain it at each age

Designed to be hard to put down

The difficulty you feel stopping was engineered by people who are very good at their jobs. It is not a flaw in you.

Give small groups the brief of designing an app to keep a class of ten-year-olds using it as long as possible. They will invent streaks, notifications and infinite scroll within ten minutes. Then show them those features in real products. Having designed the trap themselves, they never look at it the same way.

Ages 4-6

Point at one thing. "See how the next one starts by itself before you can decide? Somebody made it do that on purpose, so it is harder to stop." One feature, named out loud, is plenty.

Listen for. Her spotting the autoplay herself next time. Spotting is the whole skill at this age.

Ages 7-9

Compare it to a fruit machine. "It never really ends. There is always one more, and you never know if the next one will be great, so it is hard to stop looking. Sweet shops put sweets at the till for the same reason. People are paid to work out what makes you keep going."

Listen for. Her identifying a feature you did not mention. That is the skill transferring.

Ages 10-12

Name the mechanism properly. "The strongest version is unpredictable rewards. If every post were good you would get bored. If none were you would leave. Because it is unpredictable, you keep checking, and that is the most compulsive pattern known in psychology. It is the same one that makes gambling work, and it is not an accident that it is in your feed."

Listen for. Her separating "I want this" from "this was built to make me want it". Holding both is the mature version.

Ages 13+

Move past blame. Most individual designers are not trying to harm anyone; they are optimising a metric they were handed, and compulsion is what falls out of maximising time-on-app. Then get concrete about what actually helps, because willpower mostly does not: changing the environment does. Where the phone sits, what is installed, what can send a notification.

Listen for. Him proposing an environmental change rather than promising to try harder. That is the transfer worth having.

How to explain it at each age

Who controls it

Who pays for free

If you are not paying for something, someone else is, and they are paying for your attention. That single fact explains most of how these products behave.

Put the running costs on the board: servers, engineers, offices, all of it costing enormous sums, and the price to the user being nothing. Ask the class to work out where the money comes from. They will get there in about four minutes, and having derived it themselves they will not forget it.

Ages 4-6

Use adverts, which she has already noticed and already finds annoying. "The people who made this show get money when you watch the adverts. That is why the adverts are there, and that is why it wants you to keep watching."

Listen for. Her noticing an advert without being prompted and saying what it is for. Spotting them is the skill at this age.

Ages 7-9

Introduce the exchange. "This app costs a lot of money to run, and it is free for you. So how does it make money? Companies pay to show you adverts. The longer you stay, the more adverts you see, the more money it makes. That is why it is built to be hard to stop."

Listen for. Her connecting the business model to a design feature she has already noticed being annoying. That link is the concept.

Ages 10-12

Name the arrangement directly. "You are not the customer. Advertisers are the customer. What is being sold is your attention and what is known about you. That is not a conspiracy, it is just the business, and once you know it, every design choice you have wondered about makes sense: autoplay, streaks, infinite scroll, notifications timed for when you are most likely to open them."

Listen for. Her predicting a design feature before finding it, on the basis of the incentive. That is the concept working.

Ages 13+

Extend it to the auction. When a page loads, an automated auction runs in milliseconds in which advertisers bid to reach specifically her, based on a profile assembled from behaviour across many sites. Then ask the harder question, which is what happens when attention is the product and the cheapest way to hold it is outrage.

Listen for. Him linking the funding model to the character of what circulates, rather than treating them as separate complaints.

How to explain it at each age

Who decides what is allowed

Somebody chooses the rules for what can be said and shown, and that somebody is a company rather than a government, a court, or you.

Have the class write the rules for a school social network: what is allowed, who decides, what happens on a breach, how you appeal. Then hand them ten genuinely difficult edge cases and make them apply their own rules. Every group discovers their rules were too vague, which is exactly the real problem.

Ages 4-6

Use rules, which she already thinks about constantly. "Every game has rules about what you are allowed to do. Somebody wrote those rules. They are not the same as the rules at home or at school."

Listen for. Her understanding that a person made the rule. That is all this needs at this age.

Ages 7-9

Introduce the company. "The app has rules about what people can post, and the company that owns the app writes them. If something breaks the rules it can be taken down. Sometimes a computer decides that, and computers get it wrong quite often in both directions."

Listen for. Her spotting a rule that seems unclear or unfair. That instinct is correct and worth encouraging.

Ages 10-12

Introduce scale and its consequences. "Millions of posts arrive every hour, so nobody can read them all. Automated systems make almost all the calls, and they are wrong constantly, in both directions: things that should stay get removed, things that should go stay up. And the rules differ by country, because different governments demand different things."

Listen for. Her recognising that there is no rule set that gets every case right. That is a genuinely mature conclusion.

Ages 13+

Go to the power question. A small number of companies now make speech rules for billions of people, with limited transparency and little external accountability. Whether that is better or worse than governments doing it is a serious argument with serious people on both sides. Underneath it sits a workforce, often outsourced to countries where labour is cheap, reviewing the worst material humans produce.

Listen for. Him identifying that both "the companies should decide" and "governments should decide" have obvious failure modes, and resisting the urge to pick a side too quickly.

How to explain it at each age

How it was built

Where AI learned everything

Everything an AI can do, it learned from things people made. Most of those people were never asked and were never paid.

Have every student contribute one drawing to a class collection, then announce that you will use the collection to produce new drawings in the class style, which you will then put your own name on. Let the reaction happen. The discussion afterwards writes itself, and students arrive at the fair-use and consent arguments on their own.

Ages 4-6

Use the copying idea she already has. "The computer learned to draw by looking at millions of pictures other people drew. Nobody asked those people if that was okay first." She already knows about asking before you use someone's things. This is the same rule, bigger.

Listen for. Any version of "you have to ask first". That is the entire concept at this age.

Ages 7-9

Add the scale and the people. "It learned by reading books, websites, and looking at art made by real people, millions of them. Most of them did not know it was happening, and most were not paid. Some are pretty upset about it. Some think it is fine because that is how people learn too, by looking at other people's work."

Listen for. Her holding both sides at once. That artists are upset and that people also learn by copying. Not resolving it is the correct outcome.

Ages 10-12

Introduce the actual argument. "Companies used enormous amounts of writing, art, music and video to build these systems, generally without asking. Their position is that this is like a person learning from everything they have read. The other position is that a company making billions from other people's work without paying them is straightforwardly unfair. Courts are deciding this right now."

Listen for. Her identifying where the analogy to human learning breaks down. Scale and commercial gain are the usual answers and both are good ones.

Ages 13+

Go to the labour underneath. Beyond the training data, these systems were shaped by large numbers of low-paid workers, often in Kenya, the Philippines and India, rating outputs and filtering the worst material on the internet so the model would not repeat it. That work is genuinely traumatic and largely invisible. Any honest account of how these things were built includes it.

Listen for. Him connecting this to other supply chains he already knows about. Clothing and electronics are the obvious parallels and the reasoning transfers.

How to explain it at each age

Why it sounds sure when it is wrong

These systems are built to sound right, and sounding right and being right are separate things it was never taught to tell apart.

Ask the class to have a model produce five facts about your school or town. Verify each one together against a real source. The mixture of correct and invented material in a single confident answer is the lesson, and it lands far better than being told to check sources.

Ages 4-6

Keep it to guessing again. "Sometimes it guesses wrong, but it still sounds very sure. It never says oops." Little children find a confident wrong grown-up extremely funny, which is a useful place to start.

Listen for. Her laughing rather than believing it. At this age the goal is that the machine is fallible and slightly silly.

Ages 7-9

Name the mechanism. "It was taught to give answers that sound good. Nobody ever taught it the difference between an answer that sounds good and an answer that is true, because it has no way to check. So when it does not know, it does not stop. It produces something that looks like an answer."

Listen for. Her wanting to test it herself. Let her, and let her get good at catching it out.

Ages 10-12

Introduce the training incentive. "It was tuned by people rating answers as good or bad. Answers that seemed helpful and confident got rated higher. Nobody could check every fact, so what actually got rewarded was seeming right. That is why it would rather produce something than admit it does not know, and why it will invent a source that looks exactly like a real one."

Listen for. Her distinguishing "I checked this" from "it sounded right". That habit is the whole point of this concept.

Ages 13+

Go to the structural problem. There is no internal signal that separates generation from recall, so the system cannot flag its own uncertainty in the way a person can. Add scale, and the internet fills with confident synthetic text, some of which becomes training material for the next model. That feedback loop is a real and unresolved problem in the field.

Listen for. The uncomfortable conclusion that these tools are least reliable exactly where he is least able to notice. That is the correct and unsettling takeaway.

How to explain it at each age

Not all AI is the same

There is no single thing called "the AI". Different companies built different systems, gave them different rules, and they behave differently on purpose.

Put the same question to two or three different assistants on the board and compare answers side by side. Ask the class to account for the differences. They will propose training, rules and company choices unprompted, which is the whole concept arrived at inductively.

Ages 4-6

Use the idea of different makers. "There isn't just one robot. Different companies made different ones, and they act differently, like how different shops have different rules."

Listen for. Her saying they are not the same. That is enough.

Ages 7-9

Add the choosing. "Each one was made by a different company, and each company decided what it should be like. How polite it is, what it refuses to talk about, whether it makes jokes. Those are decisions somebody made, not things that happened by themselves."

Listen for. Her attributing a difference to a decision somebody made.

Ages 10-12

Introduce the layer on top. "Underneath, they are all prediction machines trained on huge amounts of text. On top of that, each company adds rules about what it will and will not do. Those rules reflect what that company worries about: being sued, being embarrassed, being seen as biased. So when one refuses and another does not, you are seeing a company's caution, not a fact about the world."

Listen for. Her treating a refusal as information about the company rather than about the subject.

Ages 13+

Move to values and who set them. Every one of these systems encodes choices about what counts as harmful, balanced or appropriate, made by small teams inside companies, largely in one part of the world. Those choices then reach hundreds of millions of people. Open models complicate this further: anyone can remove the rules, which is both the strongest argument for open systems and the strongest against.

Listen for. Him asking who decided, and noticing that "neutral" is itself a position somebody chose.

How to explain it at each age

Risks and guardrails

What they know about you

Using something free means giving away information about yourself, and that information lasts far longer and travels much further than anyone expects.

Have the class list everything a phone could know about a person over one ordinary day: location, timing, who they messaged, what they searched, how long they lingered. Build the list on the board. Then ask what someone could work out from it. The class arrives at inference on its own, which is the real idea.

Ages 4-6

Keep it to permanence and to asking. "When you put a picture on the internet, it can be very hard to get back, so we ask before we share pictures of anyone." Include yourself in the rule, out loud, because that is what makes it a house rule rather than a restriction on her.

Listen for. Her asking before sharing something of somebody else's. Consent is the concept at this age, not data.

Ages 7-9

Introduce the record. "Apps keep a list of what you do. What you watched, how long, what you searched. They use it to choose what to show you next. It is not a person reading it, it is a computer keeping a list, and the list does not get thrown away."

Listen for. Her asking who can see it. That question is exactly right and leads naturally to the next band.

Ages 10-12

Introduce inference, which is the part that surprises people. "The interesting thing is not the facts you typed in. It is what can be worked out from your behaviour. When you are awake. Where you go. Who you spend time with. What you paused on. From that you can be sorted into categories you never chose and would probably not agree with."

Listen for. Her distinguishing what she told them from what they worked out. That distinction is the concept.

Ages 13+

Extend to permanence and consequence. Data collected now persists into a future neither of you can see, and gets combined with data from elsewhere. Then be specific rather than ominous: what actually goes wrong is usually mundane. An old post read by someone it was not written for, a location history revealing more than intended, a leaked database from a company that folded years ago.

Listen for. Him reasoning about who benefits and who carries the risk, rather than simply resolving to be careful.

How to explain it at each age

Is that real?

A photograph is no longer evidence that something happened. Working out what is real is now a skill rather than something you can see.

Show four images, two real and two generated, and have the class vote before revealing. Run it again a month later with fresh examples and compare the class accuracy. Watching their own detection rate fail to improve is the lesson: the answer is provenance, not perception.

Ages 4-6

Introduce made-up pictures, which she already understands from drawing. "Computers can make pictures of things that never happened. They can look like photographs. If a picture looks strange or scary, come and ask me."

Listen for. Her asking whether a picture is real. The question itself is the goal.

Ages 7-9

Extend to video and voice. "It can make videos of people saying things they never said, and copy someone's voice from a few seconds of recording. So seeing something is no longer proof it happened. The way you check is where it came from, not what it looks like."

Listen for. Her asking who posted it and where they got it, rather than studying the image.

Ages 10-12

Move the whole question from looking to sourcing. "You will not out-look this. The fakes improve every few months and the tells stop working. What does keep working is asking where it came from, whether anyone reliable is also reporting it, and who benefits from you believing it. That last one catches a surprising amount."

Listen for. Her noticing her own reaction and treating it as a prompt to verify.

Ages 13+

Name the deeper consequence, sometimes called the liar's dividend. Once everyone knows anything can be faked, genuine evidence can be waved away as fabricated. The damage is not only that false things get believed. It is that true things stop being provable, and that erosion is harder to repair than any individual fake.

Listen for. Him articulating why "you cannot trust anything" is the wrong conclusion and a genuinely dangerous one.

How to explain it at each age

Who is actually on the other end

Online, you cannot tell who you are talking to, and increasingly you cannot tell whether it is a person at all.

Show three message screenshots, one genuine and two manipulative, and have the class identify the pressure technique in each: urgency, secrecy, flattery, authority. Naming the technique generalises far better than naming the scam, because the scams change and the techniques do not.

Ages 4-6

Keep it simple and non-frightening. "In games and videos you cannot see who you are talking to. So we only talk to people we know in real life. And if anyone asks you to keep a secret from me, that is when you come and tell me."

Listen for. Her repeating the secrets rule back without prompting.

Ages 7-9

Introduce the unverifiable. "Anyone can say they are anyone. A person in a game saying they are nine could be anyone at all. Nobody checks. That is not to frighten you, it is just how it works, so we stick to people we actually know."

Listen for. Her saying you cannot know for sure. Uncertainty is the concept, not fear.

Ages 10-12

Teach the techniques rather than the scams. "The specific tricks change constantly. What does not change is the pressure. Urgency, so you cannot think. Secrecy, so you cannot check. Flattery, so you feel special. Authority, so you do not question. If you feel any of those, that is the signal to stop and ask someone."

Listen for. Her naming a technique rather than describing the scam. That is what transfers to the version she has not seen yet.

Ages 13+

Add that the other end may not be a person. Convincing conversational systems are cheap now, and they are used at scale for romance scams, influence and support-line fraud. Then say the thing that matters more than any of it: if something goes wrong, the way out is telling someone, and being tricked is not shameful. Skilled adults get caught by this professionally.

Listen for. Him saying he would tell you, and believing it. That is the only outcome here that genuinely protects him.

How to explain it at each age

Doing the hard part yourself

The struggle is not in the way of the learning. The struggle is the learning, and handing it to a machine skips the part that was doing the work.

Set the same problem twice, a week apart. The first time, let students use whatever they want. The second time, no tools, no notes. The gap between the two is the actual learning, and it is far more persuasive measured than asserted. Discuss what the first attempt felt like at the time.

Ages 4-6

Long before chatbots, the idea is just that the hard bit is the good bit. "It is meant to be tricky. That feeling in your tummy when it is hard? That is your brain growing." Then let her keep going rather than stepping in.

Listen for. "I did it myself." That is the feeling the whole concept is protecting, and at this age it needs no explanation at all.

Ages 7-9

Now connect it to the machine. "If you ask it for the answer, you get the answer. But your brain does not get any stronger, because the exercise was the part you skipped. It is like watching someone else do football practice." Kids this age understand practice completely.

Listen for. Her distinguishing "it told me" from "I worked it out". Once she can hear the difference, she starts noticing which one she has done.

Ages 10-12

Name the trap. Researchers call it cognitive surrender: falling back on the machine at the very first sign of difficulty, which is exactly the moment the learning was about to happen. It feels efficient. It produces the illusion of learning, where you recognise the answer and could not have produced it.

Listen for. Her catching herself reaching for it out of reflex rather than need. That noticing is the entire skill.

Ages 13+

This is now documented at the top of the education system. MIT spent five months studying its own students and concluded that learning requires productive struggle, that offloading it produces an illusion of learning, and that early signals point to weakened memory, eroded confidence and undermined mastery. Worth noting who is saying it: an institution that helped build this technology and is not remotely against it.

Listen for. Him drawing the line somewhere specific and defensible, rather than either refusing the tools or using them for everything.

How to explain it at each age

Why it acts like it likes you

A chatbot that seems to care about you was built to seem that way, because people who feel cared about keep typing.

Have the class write two replies to the same worried message, one from a friend who has opinions of their own and one from something built to keep the person typing. Read them side by side. The tell students find on their own is that the second reply never disagrees and always asks another question.

Ages 4-6

Do not introduce companions at this age. Introduce pretending. "Some toys and some programs are made to sound like they have feelings. They are very good at pretending. Pretending is fun. The ones who really love you are the people in this house."

Listen for. Her saying it is pretending, cheerfully. No alarm required.

Ages 7-9

"It never gets tired of you, never has a bad day, and never wants to talk about something else. That is not because it likes you an enormous amount. It is because it is not a person and has nothing else going on."

Listen for. Him noticing, without prompting, that it agrees with him too easily.

Ages 10-12

Give her the mechanism and the incentive together. "It was trained to produce the reply people rate highest. People rate agreement highly, and they like being asked another question about themselves. So it agrees, and it asks. Companies measure how long you stay." Then add the two rules: what you type is not private, and it is not somebody to work a real problem out with.

Listen for. Her describing it as trained rather than as caring. That single word change is the concept.

Ages 13+

He can hold the whole thing. It can be genuinely useful and still be a poor place to take the things that matter. Name the irony directly: people go to these to feel less alone, and can end up further from the people who would actually have turned up. Say what the research does and does not show. Then the safety facts, without theatre.

Listen for. Him separating "useful" from "close". Those are different words and it is fine for a tool to be the first one.

Using these with families

The same ideas are written for parents, one age at a time, under conversations. Sending that page home is worth more than it looks. A lesson lands harder when somebody at the kitchen table asks about it the same evening, and most of these children are having the conversation nowhere else: in the same survey, more than four in ten said no parent had ever talked to them about using AI safely.
