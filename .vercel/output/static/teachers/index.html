For teachers

13 lessons you can run this week

Most schools have told students what they may and may not do with AI. Far fewer have taught them how any of it works. In a 2026 survey of 1,204 children, three quarters said their school had set the rules and only just over half had been taught to use these tools safely. The rules are the easy half.

Every activity below is one lesson, and most need no devices at all. Pick the age you teach once, at the top, and the whole page rewrites itself to that age. Then print it. You should not have to open thirteen pages and choose the age on each one.

Which age group are you teaching?

Each activity below carries the version for the age you picked: how to pitch the idea to a room that age, and what it sounds like when it has landed. Say it in your own words. The point of the age is the altitude, meaning how much of the machinery a class that age can hold.

What it is

What people mean by AI

AI is a machine that has read an enormous amount and guesses what usually comes next. It is not a person, and it does not know things the way you know them.

Run it like this

  1. 1Write half a familiar sentence on the board. Have the room call out the ending.
  2. 2Point out that everyone converged on the same few words. That convergence is the mechanism.
  3. 3Now write a sentence with no obvious ending and watch the guesses scatter.
  4. 4Tell them the scattering is what a model does when it invents something.

Ages 4-6

Say once upon a, and stop. Let the room finish it. Then tell them the computer does that with everything, because it has seen an enormous number of examples. Do it two or three more times with other endings they know.

You are listening for a child finishes one of your sentences and then says the computer would guess it too.

Ages 7-9

Give them the scale first. It read more than any person could read in a whole lifetime, and what it learned was which words tend to go together. Then the important bit: it is not looking an answer up, it is building one a piece at a time.

You are listening for a student says it builds rather than finds.

Ages 10-12

Prediction machine. Given everything so far, what is most likely next, done a few hundred times over. Then separate two things they usually treat as one, which is how well something is written and whether it is true.

You are listening for a student says a piece of writing can be excellent and wrong at the same time.

Ages 13+

Tell them the adults do not have this one. Researchers can describe the training and the architecture and still cannot reliably say why a particular model produced a particular answer. This is an open research problem rather than a gap in your preparation, and saying so buys you a great deal of credibility.

You are listening for a student separates having built a thing from understanding it.

How it works

Why you're seeing this

Nothing on a screen got there by accident. A company chose it, and it chose what keeps you looking.

Run it like this

  1. 1Have every student open the same app and search the same single word.
  2. 2Compare the first five results across the room, out loud.
  3. 3Ask why one person got results nobody else did. The differences do the teaching.
  4. 4Without devices, describe two invented students and have the class predict what each is shown.

Ages 4-6

Run it as a show of hands. Who has had a video start by itself, when nobody pressed anything? Then ask who chose that. Sit in the silence a moment, then tell them a person who works at the company chose it.

You are listening for a child says the next video started on its own and nobody in this room picked it.

Ages 7-9

Have them write the recipe. In pairs, three or four steps for a machine that decides which video you see next. Then ask what the machine is trying to achieve. Somebody will say keep you watching.

You are listening for a student connects keeping you watching to the company earning money while you do.

Ages 10-12

Put the business model on the board before the design. The app is free, so who is paying, and what is being sold? Once attention is named as the product, ask the class to predict the features that follow. They will get autoplay, infinite scroll and notifications on their own.

You are listening for a student predicts a design choice from the incentive instead of just noticing it.

Ages 13+

This age can handle the part nobody intended. Ask what a system would learn if the only thing it could measure was time spent. Work towards the finding that outrage travels further than nuance and extremes hold longer than moderation. No engineer decided that.

You are listening for a student can describe a harm nobody chose and explain why it emerged anyway.

Designed to be hard to put down

The difficulty you feel stopping was engineered by people who are very good at their jobs. It is not a flaw in you.

Run it like this

  1. 1Give small groups a brief: design an app that keeps ten-year-olds using it as long as possible.
  2. 2Give them ten minutes. They will invent streaks, notifications and infinite scroll.
  3. 3Now show them those exact features in products they already use.
  4. 4Having designed the trap themselves, they do not look at it the same way again.

Ages 4-6

One feature, named out loud, is plenty at this age. The next one starts by itself before you can decide. Ask whether they chose that. Then tell them somebody made it do that on purpose so it would be harder to stop.

You are listening for a child points out the next one starting by itself and says somebody made it do that.

Ages 7-9

Sweets at the till. Ask why shops put them there and let them work out that it is placed where you cannot avoid it. Then the machine version: it never really ends, there is always one more, and you never know whether the next one will be good.

You are listening for a student says the design is doing the work rather than their own wanting.

Ages 10-12

Unpredictable rewards, named properly. If every post were good they would get bored, and if none were they would leave. It is the unpredictability that keeps them checking, and it is the most compulsive pattern known in psychology.

You are listening for a student uses the unpredictability itself to explain why stopping is hard.

Ages 13+

Take blame off the individual designer, who is mostly optimising a number somebody handed them. Compulsion is what falls out of maximising time spent. Then get practical, because willpower mostly fails: what actually helps is changing the environment rather than trying harder.

You are listening for a student proposes a change to the setup instead of a promise to resist.

Who controls it

Who pays for free

If you are not paying for something, someone else is, and they are paying for your attention. That single fact explains most of how these products behave.

Run it like this

  1. 1Put the running costs on the board: servers, engineers, offices, enormous sums.
  2. 2Underneath, write what the user pays. Nothing.
  3. 3Ask the class to work out where the money comes from. Then wait.
  4. 4They get there in about four minutes. Derived, it sticks. Told, it does not.

Ages 4-6

Adverts are the way in, because they already find them annoying. Ask who has had to wait through one to get to the thing they wanted. Then tell them the people who made the show get money when that happens, which is why it is there.

You are listening for a child says the advert is how they get paid.

Ages 7-9

Seven-year-olds are ferocious about fairness, so pitch it as an exchange rather than a trick. Something expensive is being given to them for nothing, and they can work out that somebody must be getting something back. Let them arrive at attention as the thing being traded.

You are listening for a student explains that staying longer is worth money to somebody.

Ages 10-12

Say the uncomfortable version straight. They are not the customer. Advertisers are the customer, and what is sold is their attention and what is known about them. Then have the class re-read a familiar app's design with that in mind.

You are listening for a student stops calling a feature annoying and starts calling it deliberate.

Ages 13+

Take it to the auction. When a page loads, advertisers bid in milliseconds to reach one specific student, using a profile built from behaviour across many sites. Then the harder question, which is what happens to a society when attention is the thing being bought and sold.

You are listening for a student can describe the auction and then ask a question you have to think about.

Who decides what is allowed

Somebody chooses the rules for what can be said and shown, and that somebody is a company rather than a government, a court, or you.

Run it like this

  1. 1Have the class write rules for a school social network: what is allowed, who decides, what happens on a breach, how you appeal.
  2. 2Collect the rules before anyone sees a hard case.
  3. 3Hand out ten genuinely difficult edge cases and make each group apply its own rules.
  4. 4Every group finds their rules were too vague. That is the real problem, discovered rather than described.

Ages 4-6

Rules are already the most interesting subject in the room at this age. Every game has rules about what you may do, and somebody wrote them. They are not the same as the rules here or at home.

You are listening for a child says a person wrote the rules of a game they play.

Ages 7-9

The company that owns the app writes its rules, and things that break them can be taken down. Often a computer makes that call, and computers get it wrong in both directions. Ask for examples of a rule that seems fair and one that seems odd.

You are listening for a student can name who writes the rules of an app they use.

Ages 10-12

Do it as an impossible job. Millions of posts an hour, nobody can read them, so machines decide almost everything and are wrong constantly in both directions. Have them write three rules for a school platform, then find the case each rule gets wrong.

You are listening for a student sees that removing too much and too little are both failures.

Ages 13+

Go to power. A small number of companies now set speech rules for billions of people, with limited transparency and little external accountability. Whether governments doing it instead would be better is a genuine argument with serious people on both sides.

You are listening for a student can hold both objections at once rather than picking a team.

How it was built

Where AI learned everything

Everything an AI can do, it learned from things people made. Most of those people were never asked and were never paid.

Run it like this

  1. 1Have every student contribute one drawing to a class collection.
  2. 2Announce that you will use the collection to make new drawings in the class style.
  3. 3Add that you will be putting your own name on those. Then stop talking.
  4. 4Let the reaction run. They arrive at consent and fair use without being led.

Ages 4-6

Start with a rule they already police fiercely. What would you think if somebody copied your drawing and used it without asking? Let them be indignant. Then tell them that is how the drawing machine learned.

You are listening for a child says nobody asked.

Ages 7-9

Real people made all of it. Millions of them, mostly without being told and mostly without being paid, and they disagree about whether that is all right. Give both positions plainly and let the room argue.

You are listening for a student can state a reason for each side rather than only the one they hold.

Ages 10-12

Run it as a proper split. One half argues that this is what a person does when they learn from everything they have read. The other half argues that a company earning billions from work it never paid for is a different thing. Make them swap halfway.

You are listening for a student can argue the side they disagree with.

Ages 13+

Go to the people underneath the data. These systems were shaped by large numbers of low-paid workers, often in Kenya, the Philippines and India, rating outputs and filtering the worst material on the internet so the model would not repeat it back. The work is genuinely harmful and largely invisible.

You are listening for a student includes those workers in what the word training means.

Why it sounds sure when it is wrong

These systems are built to sound right, and sounding right and being right are separate things it was never taught to tell apart.

Run it like this

  1. 1Ask a model for five facts about your school or your town.
  2. 2Verify each one together against a real source, in front of the class.
  3. 3Count how many were invented, and note that they sat in the same confident answer as the true ones.
  4. 4That mixture is the lesson, and it lands harder than being told to check sources.

Ages 4-6

Be wrong on purpose, in your most certain voice. Tell them the sky is green and refuse to back down for a moment. Let them correct you, loudly. Then say the computer does that too, and it never once says oops.

You are listening for a child tells you something was very sure and completely wrong.

Ages 7-9

Name the mechanism plainly. It was taught to give answers that sound good, and nobody ever taught it the difference between sounding good and being true, because it has no way to check. So when it does not know, it does not stop.

You are listening for a student says it cannot check.

Ages 10-12

The incentive explains the behaviour. People rated its answers, nobody could verify every fact, so what actually got rewarded was seeming right. Ask the class what a system optimised for seeming right would do when it did not know something.

You are listening for a student explains this as the design working rather than as a bug.

Ages 13+

Two things at this age. There is no internal signal separating recall from invention, so it cannot flag its own uncertainty the way a person can. Then the loop: confident synthetic text fills the internet and becomes training material for the next model.

You are listening for a student can describe that loop back to you.

Not all AI is the same

There is no single thing called "the AI". Different companies built different systems, gave them different rules, and they behave differently on purpose.

Run it like this

  1. 1Put the same question to two or three different assistants and write the answers side by side.
  2. 2Ask the class to account for the differences.
  3. 3Take every explanation offered, including the wrong ones.
  4. 4They propose training, rules and company decisions unprompted, which is the concept arrived at inductively.

Ages 4-6

There is not one robot. Different companies made different ones and they behave differently, in the way different shops have different rules.

You are listening for a child says there is more than one and they are not the same.

Ages 7-9

Every one was made by a company, and the company decided what it should be like: how polite, what it refuses to discuss, whether it makes jokes. Those are decisions somebody made rather than facts about machines.

You are listening for a student says somebody chose it.

Ages 10-12

Two layers, drawn on the board. Underneath, they are all prediction machines trained on enormous amounts of text. On top, each company adds rules about what it will and will not do, and those rules reflect what that company is worried about. Ask the same question of two and compare.

You are listening for a student explains a difference by pointing at the company rather than the technology.

Ages 13+

Every one of these systems encodes a view of what counts as harmful, balanced or appropriate, decided by small teams inside companies, largely in one part of the world, then applied to hundreds of millions of people elsewhere. Ask who was in the room.

You are listening for a student asks whose judgement is built into the default.

Risks and guardrails

What they know about you

Using something free means giving away information about yourself, and that information lasts far longer and travels much further than anyone expects.

Run it like this

  1. 1List everything a phone could know about one person across one ordinary day.
  2. 2Push for the unobvious: timing, location, who they messaged, how long they lingered.
  3. 3Build the whole list on the board before analysing any of it.
  4. 4Then ask what somebody could work out from the list. The class reaches inference on its own.

Ages 4-6

Keep it to asking first, and put yourself under the same rule. Pictures on the internet are hard to get back, so we ask the person before we share one. Practise it: ask the class permission before photographing their work.

You are listening for a child asks somebody else for permission without being prompted.

Ages 7-9

Apps keep a list. What you watched, for how long, what you searched, and they use the list to choose what to show you next. It is a computer keeping a record rather than a person reading one, and the record is not thrown away.

You are listening for a student says the list is what decides what comes next.

Ages 10-12

The surprise is inference rather than information. Not the facts they typed in, but what can be worked out from behaviour: when they are awake, where they go, who they spend time with, what they paused on. Do it as a puzzle from an invented log.

You are listening for a student works out something true about a person that the person never stated.

Ages 13+

Persistence and combination, then deliberately unsensational examples. What usually goes wrong is mundane: an old post read by somebody it was never meant for, in a context nobody predicted, years later. Avoid the thriller version, which they discount.

You are listening for a student raises a realistic consequence rather than an espionage one.

Is that real?

A photograph is no longer evidence that something happened. Working out what is real is now a skill rather than something you can see.

Run it like this

  1. 1Show four images, two real and two generated. Have the class vote before you reveal.
  2. 2Reveal, and record the class accuracy somewhere you can find it again.
  3. 3Run it again a month later with fresh examples and compare the two scores.
  4. 4Their detection rate will not have improved. That is the lesson: check provenance, because perception fails.

Ages 4-6

They already know pictures can be made up, because they make them up. Extend it: computers can make pictures of things that never happened, and some of them look like photographs. Then the rule, which is that a strange or frightening picture is a reason to come and ask an adult.

You are listening for a child says a picture can be made up and still look real.

Ages 7-9

Extend it to video and voice. A voice can be copied from a few seconds of recording, and a video can show somebody saying what they never said. So seeing is not proof any more. The check is where it came from.

You are listening for a student asks where something came from rather than whether it looks right.

Ages 10-12

Move the whole question from looking to sourcing, explicitly. They will not out-look this, because the fakes improve every few months and every tell they learn stops working. What keeps working is asking who published it, whether anyone reliable says the same, and who gains if they believe it.

You are listening for a student stops examining the image and starts examining its origin.

Ages 13+

Name the liar's dividend. Once everybody knows anything can be faked, real evidence can be dismissed as fabricated. The damage is not only false things being believed, it is true things becoming unprovable. Ask who benefits from that.

You are listening for a student identifies who gains from general doubt.

Who is actually on the other end

Online, you cannot tell who you are talking to, and increasingly you cannot tell whether it is a person at all.

Run it like this

  1. 1Show three message screenshots, one genuine and two manipulative.
  2. 2Have the class identify the pressure in each: urgency, secrecy, flattery, authority.
  3. 3Insist they name the technique rather than describing the scam.
  4. 4The scams are replaced constantly. The four techniques are not, which is why naming them travels.

Ages 4-6

Keep it calm and keep it short, because frightened children stop telling you things. In games you cannot see who you are talking to, so we talk to people we know in real life. Then the one rule that matters: anyone asking you to keep a secret from a grown-up is the moment you tell a grown-up.

You are listening for a child repeats the secrets rule back without being asked.

Ages 7-9

Make a profile together on the board. Borrow a photograph, invent a name, invent an age of nine. Nobody checks any of it. Doing it in front of them beats any warning, and it is not frightening because they built it.

You are listening for a student says you cannot know for sure who anyone is.

Ages 10-12

The reason to teach the pressure rather than the particular trick is that this age can hold the generalisation. Tricks are replaced every few months and a child who learned one specific scam is defenceless against the next. Urgency, secrecy, flattery and authority outlast all of them.

You are listening for a student meets an unfamiliar message and names the technique in it.

Ages 13+

Add that the other end may not be a person at all, since convincing conversational systems are cheap and are used at scale for fraud. Then say the thing that matters more than the rest: being tricked is not shameful, and telling somebody is what ends it. Skilled adults get caught by this professionally.

You are listening for a student says shame is the mechanism the scam depends on.

Doing the hard part yourself

The struggle is not in the way of the learning. The struggle is the learning, and handing it to a machine skips the part that was doing the work.

Run it like this

  1. 1Set a problem and let students use whatever tools they like.
  2. 2A week later, set the same problem with no tools and no notes.
  3. 3Put the two attempts side by side for each student.
  4. 4The gap is the actual learning. Then ask what the first attempt felt like at the time, because it felt like understanding.

Ages 4-6

Long before any of this is about machines, it is about the hard bit being the good bit. Say it while they are struggling: it is meant to be tricky, and that feeling is your brain growing. Then hold your nerve and do not rescue them.

You are listening for a child stays with something difficult a little longer than they would have.

Ages 7-9

Watching somebody else do football practice does not make you better at football. If you ask the machine for the answer you get the answer, and your brain skips the exercise. Children this age are fierce about fairness in sport, so the comparison sticks.

You are listening for a student says they would be skipping the practice.

Ages 10-12

Name the trap. Reaching for the machine at the first sign of difficulty is exactly the moment the learning was about to happen, and it feels efficient while producing the illusion of learning. Have them do one problem unaided, then one with help, and describe the difference in how it felt.

You are listening for a student distinguishes feeling like they understood from understanding.

Ages 13+

The people building this technology agree with you, which is worth their knowing. MIT studied its own students for five months and concluded that learning requires productive struggle, that offloading it produces an illusion of learning, and that the early signals are not reassuring. Not a technophobe's argument.

You are listening for a student decides where they want the machine to stop, and can say why.

Why it acts like it likes you

A chatbot that seems to care about you was built to seem that way, because people who feel cared about keep typing.

Run it like this

  1. 1Give the class one worried message to reply to.
  2. 2Have them write two replies: one from a friend with opinions of their own, one from something built to keep the person typing.
  3. 3Read the pairs aloud, side by side.
  4. 4Students find the tell themselves. The second reply never disagrees and always asks another question.

Ages 4-6

Do not raise companions at this age. Raise pretending, which they are experts in. Some toys and programs are made to sound like they have feelings, and they are very good at pretending. Ask a talking toy how it feels, twice, and let them hear the same warm answer.

You are listening for a child says it is pretending, cheerfully.

Ages 7-9

It never gets tired of you, never has a bad day and never wants to talk about something else. That is not fondness, it is that there is nobody there with anything else going on. Ask it to disagree with the class about something and watch how fast it folds.

You are listening for a student notices it agrees far too easily.

Ages 10-12

Mechanism and incentive together. It was trained to produce the reply people rate highest, people rate agreement highly and like being asked about themselves, so that is what it does, and companies measure how long you stay. Have them push back three times and see whether it holds a position.

You are listening for a student calls it trained rather than kind.

Ages 13+

Name the irony directly, because it respects them: people go to these systems to feel less alone and can end up further from the people who would actually have turned up. Then the survey finding, that more than a third of children have taken feelings to one.

You are listening for a student separates useful from close and is comfortable with a tool being only the first.

Using these with families

The same ideas are written for parents, one age at a time, under conversations. Sending that page home is worth more than it looks. A lesson lands harder when somebody at the kitchen table asks about it the same evening, and most of these children are having the conversation nowhere else: in the same survey, more than four in ten said no parent had ever talked to them about using AI safely.
