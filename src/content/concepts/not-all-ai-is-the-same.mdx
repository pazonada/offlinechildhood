title: Not all AI is the same
idea: There is no single thing called "the AI". Different companies built different systems, gave them different rules, and they behave differently on purpose.
description: Stops a kid treating any one system as the voice of the machine, which is the assumption that makes everything else it says harder to question.
order: 11
strand: How it was built
misconceptions:
  - >-
    That all these tools are the same underneath. They differ in training data,
    in the rules layered on top, and in what the company wanted them to be like.
  - >-
    That the personality is a personality. The friendliness was designed, tested
    and chosen, in the same way a shop's greeting is.
  - >-
    That refusing to answer means the topic is forbidden. It usually means one
    company drew a line there, and another company drew it elsewhere.
  - >-
    Adults describe "what AI thinks" as though there were one opinion. There is
    not.

  - >-

  - >-

  - >-

    inClass: >-

      A student saying somebody chose it.
    explain: >-
      Add the choosing. "Each one was made by a different company, and each

      to talk about, whether it makes jokes. Somebody made every one of those decisions. None of it happened by itself."
    tryThis: >-
      Ask two of them the same question and compare. Then ask her which answer she
      preferred and why, which is a better question than which was right.
    listenFor: >-

    ifMoreExperienced: >-
      Ask her to guess what kind of person a company was imagining when they made
      it sound the way it does.
  - band: '10-12'
    inClass: >-
      Two layers, drawn on the board. Underneath, they are all prediction machines
      trained on enormous amounts of text. On top, each company adds rules about
      what it will and will not do, and those rules reflect what that company is
      worried about. Ask the same question of two and compare.
    listenForClass: >-
      A student explaining a difference by pointing at the company rather than the
      technology.
    explain: >-
      Introduce the layer on top. "Underneath, they are all prediction machines
      trained on huge amounts of text. On top of that, each company adds rules
      about what it will and will not do. Those rules reflect what that company
      worries about: being sued, being embarrassed, being seen as biased. So when
      one refuses and another does not, the difference is the companies' caution. The world stayed the same."
    tryThis: >-
      Find a question one assistant declines and another answers. Ask her what she
      thinks each company was worried about.
    listenFor: >-
      Her treating a refusal as information about the company rather than about
      the subject.
    ifMoreExperienced: >-
      Introduce the idea that the same underlying model can behave very
      differently depending on the instructions wrapped around it.
  - band: '13+'
    inClass: >-
      Every one of these systems encodes a view of what counts as harmful, balanced
      or appropriate, decided by small teams inside companies, largely in one part
      of the world, then applied to hundreds of millions of people elsewhere. Ask
      who was in the room.
    listenForClass: >-
      A student asking whose judgement is built into the default.
    explain: >-
      Move to values and who set them. Every one of these systems encodes choices
      about what counts as harmful, balanced or appropriate, made by small teams
      inside companies, largely in one part of the world. Those choices then reach

      can remove the rules, which is both the strongest argument for open systems
      and the strongest against.
    tryThis: >-
      Have him probe the same politically contested question across several

      more revealing than any single answer.
    listenFor: >-
      Him asking who decided, and noticing that "neutral" is itself a position
      somebody chose.
    ifMoreExperienced: >-
      Discuss whether a neutral assistant is possible, and what it would

## Why the plural matters

A child who says "the AI said" has already conceded the argument. It sounds like
a single authority, the way people once said "the encyclopaedia said".

A child who says "ChatGPT said, and Claude said something different" is doing
something else entirely. They are treating each one as a product made by a
company with its own reasons, which is exactly what it is.

That habit costs nothing to build and it changes how every future answer gets
received.

## The personality was designed

Worth saying plainly, because it is not obvious: the warmth is a product
decision. Someone chose how friendly it should be, tested versions, and picked
one.

That is not sinister. Shops train staff to be pleasant for the same reasons. But a
child who knows the friendliness was designed is much less likely to mistake it
for a relationship, and that matters more every year as these systems get better
at sounding like they care.
