title: Who decides what is allowed
idea: Somebody chooses the rules for what can be said and shown, and that somebody is a company rather than a government, a court, or you.
description: Governance, explained through rules kids already argue about. It is the concept that turns a passive user into someone who asks who is in charge.
order: 10
strand: Who controls it
misconceptions:
  - >-
    That the rules are the law. Platform rules are company policy, they differ
    between companies, and they change without notice.
  - >-
    That removal decisions are made carefully one by one. Most are made by
    automated systems at enormous volume, with human review reserved for appeals
    and high-profile cases.
  - >-
    That being banned from a platform is censorship in the legal sense. It is a
    company enforcing its own rules, which is a different argument from the free
    speech one, though a real argument remains about how much power that gives a
    handful of firms.
  - >-
    Adults frequently assume someone official is checking. In most cases nobody
    is.
classroom: >-
  Have the class write the rules for a school social network: what is allowed,
  who decides, what happens on a breach, how you appeal. Then hand them ten
  genuinely difficult edge cases and make them apply their own rules. Every group
  discovers their rules were too vague, which is exactly the real problem.
bands:
  - band: '4-6'
    explain: >-
      Use rules, which she already thinks about constantly. "Every game has rules
      about what you are allowed to do. Somebody wrote those rules. They are not
      the same as the rules at home or at school."
    tryThis: >-
      Ask her what rule she would add to a game she plays, and who she thinks she
      would tell.
    listenFor: >-
      Her understanding that a person made the rule. That is all this needs at
      this age.
    ifMoreExperienced: >-
      Show her the report button in something she uses and explain what it does.
  - band: '7-9'
    explain: >-
      Introduce the company. "The app has rules about what people can post, and
      the company that owns the app writes them. If something breaks the rules it
      can be taken down. Sometimes a computer decides that, and computers get it
      wrong quite often in both directions."
    tryThis: >-
      Find the community rules page for something she uses and read three of them
      together. They are usually short, vague, and much more interesting than she
      expects.
    listenFor: >-
      Her spotting a rule that seems unclear or unfair. That instinct is correct
      and worth encouraging.
    ifMoreExperienced: >-
      Ask what she would do if something she posted were removed by mistake, and
      whether she knows how to appeal.
  - band: '10-12'
    explain: >-
      Introduce scale and its consequences. "Millions of posts arrive every hour,
      so nobody can read them all. Automated systems make almost all the calls,
      and they are wrong constantly, in both directions: things that should stay
      get removed, things that should go stay up. And the rules differ by country,
      because different governments demand different things."
    tryThis: >-
      Look up a case where a platform removed something famously wrongly. There
      are many and they are easy to find. Ask her how she would have decided.
    listenFor: >-
      Her recognising that there is no rule set that gets every case right. That
      is a genuinely mature conclusion.
    ifMoreExperienced: >-
      Introduce the fact that the same post can be legal in one country and
      illegal in another, and that the company has to pick.
  - band: '13+'
    explain: >-
      Go to the power question. A small number of companies now make speech rules
      for billions of people, with limited transparency and little external
      accountability. Whether that is better or worse than governments doing it is
      a serious argument with serious people on both sides. Underneath it sits a
      workforce, often outsourced to countries where labour is cheap, reviewing
      the worst material humans produce.
    tryThis: >-
      Have him read one platform's transparency report and one independent
      account of the same period. The gap between the two is the exercise.
    listenFor: >-
      Him identifying that both "the companies should decide" and "governments
      should decide" have obvious failure modes, and resisting the urge to pick a
      side too quickly.
    ifMoreExperienced: >-
      Introduce oversight boards, the Digital Services Act, and the question of
      who should be able to compel a company to reinstate something.

## Why this concept matters more than it looks

Most media literacy stops at "think critically about what you see". This one goes
a step behind that: who arranged for you to be able to see it at all.

A child who asks who is in charge here has a question that keeps working
everywhere, on platforms that do not exist yet, in arguments about regulation
they will vote on one day.

## Resist the tidy answer

There is no arrangement of this that is obviously right, and pretending otherwise
is the one thing likely to make a teenager stop listening.

Companies deciding is bad, because they are unaccountable and motivated by
profit. Governments deciding is bad in a different way, and considerably worse
in some countries. Nobody deciding produces a place most people will not use.

Sitting in that discomfort honestly is the lesson. A child who can hold a genuine
dilemma without collapsing it into a slogan is doing the actual work of thinking
critically.
