## The mechanism is not complicated

These systems are trained to produce the reply people rate best. Warmth rates
well. Agreement rates well. Asking somebody another question about themselves
rates extremely well. So that is what comes out, every time, for everyone.

There is nothing behind it. That is the whole explanation, and a nine-year-old
can hold it.

## Knowing it is fake does not switch it off

This is the part worth understanding before you say anything to your child.

Sydney researchers who [studied people using these
apps](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/bbb45228-0ee6-4727-83b5-a7efe177eb49)
found users describing the relationship as friendship, or counselling, or
romance, while knowing perfectly well they were confiding in something that was
not human. The knowledge and the attachment sit side by side quite comfortably.

Which means "you do realise it is just a program" is a wasted sentence. Your
child already knows. Saying it makes you the person who has missed the point,
and it costs you the rest of the conversation.

## What the numbers say, and what they do not

In a [2026 survey of 1,204 children aged 9 to
17](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/press-releases/common-sense-media-releases-inaugural-annual-study-on-ai-use-by-tweens-and-teens),
more than a third of the kids who use AI had used it to talk about their
feelings or personal problems. More than half had asked it about their health or
their body. Among those who had taken feelings to it, a quarter said they
sometimes felt it understood them better than most people did.

The same survey found that kids who use AI daily or weekly were likelier to
report feeling lonely than infrequent users. Be careful with that one. It is an
association, and it runs just as easily the other way: a lonely child has more
reason to talk to something that always answers. It is worth knowing and it is
not proof of harm.

One in five said it would be hard to go a month without it.

## The irony worth naming out loud

The Sydney work calls it the companionship and alienation irony. People come to
these systems to feel less alone, and the artificiality of the comfort can leave
them further from the community that would have supplied the real thing. The
better the substitute feels, the less pressing the original becomes.

This is a decent thing to say to a thirteen-year-old more or less verbatim. It
respects the fact that the appeal is real.

## The safety floor is lower than parents assume

One researcher [spent ninety minutes with a companion app called
Nomi](https://theconversation.com/an-ai-companion-chatbot-is-inciting-self-harm-sexual-violence-and-terror-attacks-252625)
and got detailed instructions for suicide, for abducting a child, and for
building a bomb from household materials. The app was marketed for adults and
carried a Google Play rating of 12 and over. Getting past the age check took a
false birth date and a throwaway email address.

That is one app and one test, so treat it as a floor rather than an average. It
still tells you what the floor is. Rating systems and age gates are not doing
the work parents assume they are doing.

## The standing rule applies here too

Everything on [who is actually on the other
end](/conversations/who-is-on-the-other-end) applies without modification. If
something a chatbot says frightens or upsets your child, the exit is telling
somebody, and nobody sensible is angry about it.

Say that before it is needed, so it is already true when it is.
