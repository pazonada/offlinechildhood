title: Why you're seeing this
idea: Nothing on a screen got there by accident. A company chose it, usually to make money by making you keep looking.
description: The first idea in the whole curriculum. Once a kid can ask "why am I being shown this", every other question gets easier.
order: 2
strand: How it works
misconceptions:
  - >-
    That the app shows everyone the same thing. It does not. Two kids sitting side
    by side see different feeds, which is exactly why comparing notes works so well
    as a demonstration.
  - >-
    That it shows you what you like. Closer, but not right. It shows what holds
    attention, and the things that hold attention are not always the things a
    person would have chosen.
  - >-
    That someone at the company picked this particular video for you. A person
    wrote the rules and a machine applies them billions of times, which is why
    nobody at the company can fully explain any single recommendation.
  - >-
    Adults get this one wrong too. Plenty of grown-ups still treat their feed as a
    neutral window on the world rather than a product decision.

bands:
  - band: '4-6'
    inClass: >-
      Run it as a show of hands. Who has had a video start by itself, when nobody
      pressed anything? Then ask who chose that. Sit in the silence a moment, then
      tell them a person who works at the company chose it.
    listenForClass: >-
      A child saying the next video started on its own, and nobody in the room chose
      it.
    explain: >-
      Skip the word "algorithm". Try this instead: "Whoever made this show wants you
      to keep watching, so they made the next one start all by itself. They chose that for you, without asking you." Keep it to the fact that a person made a choice,
      because that is the whole idea at this age.
    tryThis: >-
      Watch something together and pause right when the next episode auto-starts.
      Ask: "Did you pick that? Who did?" Then let her pick the next one out loud,
      so the difference between being handed something and choosing it is
      something she has felt rather than been told.
    listenFor: >-
      Any version of "somebody wanted me to." She needs to know a person is on
      the other end of the machine.
    ifMoreExperienced: >-
      If she already uses a tablet alone, add one rule with the explanation
      attached: "We turn off the part that picks for you, so you get to pick."
  - band: '7-9'
    inClass: >-
      Have them write the recipe. In pairs, three or four steps for a machine that
      decides which video you see next. Then ask what the machine is trying to
      achieve. Somebody will say keep you watching.
    listenForClass: >-
      A student connecting the watching to the money the company makes while you
      watch.
    explain: >-
      Now you can name it. "An algorithm is a set of instructions, like a recipe.
      People wrote a recipe that decides which video you see next. The recipe's
      job is to keep you watching, because the company earns money from ads while
      you watch." Seven-year-olds understand recipes and they understand rules,
      which is all this requires.
    tryThis: >-
      Sit with two different accounts, yours and hers, and search the same word.
      Compare the top five. The results differ, and she will notice on her own,
      which is worth a great deal more than being told.
    listenFor: >-
      "Why did you get different ones than me?" That question means the idea has
      landed and you can stop explaining.
    ifMoreExperienced: >-
      If she already has her own account, look at the recommendations together and
      have her guess which of her past videos caused each one. She will be right
      more often than you expect.
  - band: '10-12'
    inClass: >-
      Put the business model on the board before the design. The app is free, so who
      is paying, and what is being sold? Once attention is named as the product, ask
      the class to predict the features that follow. They will get autoplay,
      infinite scroll and notifications on their own.
    listenForClass: >-
      A student predicting a design choice from the incentive before anyone points it out.
    explain: >-
      Introduce the money. "These apps are free, so you are not the customer.
      Advertisers are the customer, and your attention is the product being sold.
      Nobody is conspiring. That is the business model, and it explains almost every design choice." Then name the choices: autoplay,
      infinite scroll, streaks, the pull to refresh, notifications timed for when
      you are most likely to open them.
    tryThis: >-
      Have her find the design feature in an app she uses that exists purely to
      extend a session. Then have her turn one of them off and use the app for a
      week. Ask her afterwards whether she used it less, and whether she missed it.
    listenFor: >-
      Her spotting a design choice you did not point out. That is the skill
      transferring, and it is the point of the whole exercise.
    ifMoreExperienced: >-
      For a kid already deep in a platform, go one level further: ask who benefits
      from each recommendation, and whether the creator, the advertiser and the
      viewer want the same thing.
  - band: '13+'
    inClass: >-
      This age can handle the part nobody intended. Ask what a system would learn if
      the only thing it could measure was time spent. Work towards the finding that
      outrage travels further than nuance and extremes hold longer than moderation.
      No engineer decided that.
    listenForClass: >-
      A student describing a harm nobody chose, and explaining why it emerged
      anyway.
    explain: >-
      Engagement optimization has second-order effects. A system tuned for
      attention will favor outrage over nuance, extremes over moderation, and
      that a body-comparison spiral is very engaging indeed. No engineer sat down and decided this. It emerged from
      the objective, and that is a more unsettling fact than a villain would be.
    tryThis: >-
      Have him deliberately train a fresh account for a week, engaging only with
      one narrow topic, then look at what the feed became. Doing it on purpose is
      how you see the mechanism you cannot see when it happens to you.
    listenFor: >-
      Recognition that the system has no intent. The sophisticated version drops the evil company for
      something stranger: an optimizer that does not care.
    ifMoreExperienced: >-
      Move to the labor and moderation layer: who decides what is allowed, who is
      paid to look at the worst of it, and what happens in the countries where
      those decisions are cheapest to make.

## The question that works on everything

A kid who can ask "why am I being shown this" has a tool that works on everything
she will meet later: recommendation feeds, targeted ads, chatbots, whatever
replaces them. Everything else in this curriculum is a variation on it.

It also has a property most media literacy lessons lack. It is checkable. She can
run the comparison herself and see the difference with her own eyes, which means
she is not taking your word for it. That matters more than it sounds, because a
child who has verified one thing about how technology works is much harder to
convince that the rest is magic.

## The mistake to avoid

Do not turn this into a lesson about how the apps are bad. It backfires, and it
is also not quite true. The point is that a system was built with a goal, which is not the same as her
goal. Understanding that keeps her curious, and you want a curious kid rather
than a kid who hides the device from you because you told her the internet is
poison.
