## The thing that changed in August

On 10 August 2026, Google [began rolling out Gemini in
Classroom](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/08/gemini-in-google-classroom-is-expanding-to-users-of-all-ages-with-contextualized-Gemini-starter-prompts-for-students.html)
to students of all ages. The sentence that matters is Google's own: access to
the Gemini tab in Classroom is on by default for teachers and students of all
ages.

On by default. Not offered, not opted into, not announced to you.

Access is controlled by whoever administers your school's Google account. There
is no teacher-level switch, so the teacher standing in front of your child may
have had no say in it and may not know it happened. There is no parental
notification in the announcement at all. If a school wants it off for under
eighteens, an administrator has to build a separate organisational unit and turn
it off there.

So a reasonable thing to assume is that your child has a conversational AI inside
their school account right now, and that nobody told either of you.

## What to actually ask

Three questions, in an email, to whoever runs IT rather than to the class
teacher, who probably cannot answer them:

Which AI tools are enabled on my child's account. Who can see what my child types
into them. And is there a way to opt my child out.

Ask in writing. Not to be difficult, but because the answer tends to be more
precise when somebody has to type it, and because you will want to know what you
were told when it changes again.

## Where the evidence actually points, including against me

It is tempting to say these machines are bad for learning. The research does not
support that, and this site is not going to pretend otherwise.

The best pooled evidence on giving every child a laptop [found small positive
effects](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316628645) on achievement in English,
writing, maths and science, in the region of 0.16 standard deviations. Small, and
much smaller than things like tutoring, but positive. Only ten studies were solid
enough to pool, so it is a thin base. It is still the opposite of the claim that
the devices are making children worse at school.

The sharper finding is about what the device replaces. Across 54 studies and more
than 170,000 readers, [comprehension was reliably better on paper than on
screen](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.003), and the gap grew over the
years rather than closing as screens improved. It is worst for informational text
and worst when the reader is working against the clock, which describes most
reading a school sets.

So the honest version is narrower than the complaint and more useful than it. The
laptop is roughly fine as a laptop. It is a poor substitute for a book, and a
school that has quietly moved its reading onto screens has traded away something
measurable for something convenient.

That is a question you can ask, and it lands better than a general objection to
technology, because it is specific and there is evidence behind it.

## The part that is about the company, not the child

Chromebooks are in schools at the scale they are because they are cheap to buy
and cheap to administer, which are procurement virtues rather than educational
ones. It is worth being clear-eyed that this is a purchasing decision that
happened to become a pedagogical one.

It has consequences beyond the classroom. Denmark's data protection regulator
examined how student data reached Google through these devices, concluded there
was no legal basis for all of the purposes involved, and ordered 53 municipalities
to change what they were doing. That is a regulator, not a campaign group, and it
is about data rather than about learning. Both things can be true: the device is
adequate for schoolwork, and the arrangement around it deserves more scrutiny
than a purchase order gets.

## What is still yours

Not much of the device. Quite a lot of the surroundings.

Where it charges is yours. Whether it goes into a bedroom is yours. Whether your
child understands what the assistant inside it actually is, and why it agrees
with them so readily, is entirely yours, and is covered in [why it acts like it
likes you](/conversations/acts-like-it-likes-you).

And the reading is more yours than it feels. A child who does the set reading on
paper is not opting out of school. They are taking the version the evidence
prefers.
