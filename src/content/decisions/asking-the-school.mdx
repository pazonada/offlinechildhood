## You are not the radical here

Start with the fact that changes the whole conversation: as of mid-2026, [at
least 39 states and the District of
Columbia](https://www.edweek.org/technology/which-states-ban-or-restrict-cellphones-in-schools/2024/06)
require school districts to ban or restrict student phone use, and most of that
legislation passed inside two years. Legislatures do not move that fast for
fringe positions.

So walk in calibrated. A parent asking a school to think harder about its
technology in 2026 is not the eccentric at the gate. You are asking your school
to catch up with the direction most of the country has already picked, and you
are allowed to say so.

## Find out who holds the switch

The first practical step is finding out who decided, because it is rarely who
you think, and complaints aimed at the wrong desk evaporate.

The teacher standing in front of your child usually decided nothing. When
Google turned on its AI assistant for students of all ages, it went on by
default, controlled only by whoever administers the school's account, with no
teacher-level switch at all. The same pattern holds for most of what arrives on
a school device: a tablet that turns up with news notifications enabled, a
browser that goes anywhere, an AI tool nobody mentioned. Somebody chose each of
those, and somebody can unchoose them.

At a private school, the head of school can change policy over a weekend, and parents are
the paying customers whose opinion the board exists to hear. At a public
school, the road runs through the district: an IT department made the
purchasing decision, and the school board is where purchasing decisions get
questioned in public. Aim the ask accordingly, and copy the person who can act
on it.

## Ask for the grantable things first

Whatever your eventual goal, open with requests a school can say yes to without
conceding it was wrong. These get granted, and each one makes the next
conversation easier.

The list writes itself once you look at a school device with the site's usual
question, which is who chose this and what is it for. Notifications off, all of
them, because no child needs a news alert mid-lesson. AI assistants off by
default, opted into deliberately if a teacher wants them. The browser limited
to what the work needs. And assigned reading available on paper, which is the
one request with the cleanest evidence behind it: [across 54
studies](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.003), comprehension was
reliably better on paper than on screen, and the gap widened as screens
improved.

A school that says yes to those has fixed most of what was wrong without a
single device leaving the building.

## Numbers move schools

The math from the group-chat problem applies here at a larger scale. One
parent raising screens can be seen as a personality problem. Three families
raising the same concern, in writing, within a term, is a pattern the principal has
to manage, and five is an agenda item at the next board meeting.

Finding those families is easier than it feels, because most of them are
waiting for somebody else to go first. The survey evidence gives you a neutral
opening: schools have been good at setting rules for AI and far worse at
teaching anybody to use it safely, and most kids say no adult has ever walked
them through it. That framing asks the school to finish a job, and no parent
who hears it thinks you are the zealot.

## What to say

If you would rather have the writing done for you, the [advocacy
builder](/advocate) turns your goals into a printable handout: your requests,
the evidence for each, and the script, plus the questions that show whether
anyone at the school is minding the switches at all.

To the principal or the IT lead, in writing:

> "Which AI tools and notifications are enabled on the devices our children
> use, who reviewed those defaults, and can we opt out? We are asking because
> we would rather work with the school on this than around it."

At a school board meeting, where you get two minutes:

> "Thirty-nine states now restrict phones in schools. We are not asking this
> district to lead the country. We are asking it to review what is switched on
> by default on the devices it hands our children, and to tell parents the
> reasons."

To another parent, which is where the whole thing actually starts:

> "Have you looked at what the school tablet has switched on? I am going to ask
> about it. Want to sign the email with me?"

## Expect reasons, and give them

Rules need justifications. Ask the school for the reason behind each default,
in the same breath as the ask. A school that can explain why the browser is
open has thought about it, and you have learned something. A school that
cannot has answered a different question, and now knows it owes one. Offer
your reasons just as plainly: you want the request to arrive calmly with
evidence so you can be read as a partner, rather than a furious parent to be
managed.

## When this doesn't apply

A family that chose a deliberately tech-forward school signed up for the
defaults, and in that case, the conversation should happen before enrollment
instead of after it.

If a teacher at the school is already pushing this from inside, and one often
is, your job changes from critic to reinforcement. Ask them what request from
parents would help them most, and make that one.

And a family with no capacity for any of this is not failing. The
[school-issued device](/decisions/school-issued-devices) page covers the end of
this you control from your own kitchen, and it works whether or not the school
ever changes a thing.
