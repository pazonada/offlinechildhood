## The example is most of the teaching

Long before a child can hold an argument about screens, they have absorbed a
model of what the object is for. Where it sits at dinner. Whether it wins when it
buzzes mid-sentence. What a parent's face does in a quiet moment: settles, or
drops to a glow.

Every script on this site leans on this already. The bedroom rule works when
"mine is out here too" is true. The hard-to-put-down conversation lands when you
can say "right up until I do" about yourself without wincing. You cannot teach
[why it is hard to put down](/conversations/hard-to-put-down) while visibly
unable to put yours down, and children notice the gap between rule and practice
years before they can name it.

## What the evidence shows

Researchers call it technoference: technology interrupting time between parent
and child. In [a study of 170 American
families](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12822) with children averaging three years
old, parents who rated their own phone use as a problem reported more of those
interruptions, and more interruptions tracked with more behaviour problems in
the child.

Hold it as lightly as this site holds every other correlation. Parents reported
on themselves, and the arrow can run both ways, because a child having a hard
week also sends a parent to the phone. What the study establishes is smaller and
still useful: the parent's phone belongs in any honest conversation about the
child's.

## This is not a virtue problem

The same design that makes the feed hard for a twelve-year-old to leave is
working on you, tuned by the same measurements. Noticing that you check your
phone forty times an evening is not a discovery about your character.

Which is why the advice here is environmental rather than moral, exactly as it
is for kids. A phone with a fixed spot gets visited instead of carried. A
grayscale screen is a duller slot machine. Notifications cut to actual humans
turn the phone back into a phone. None of this requires willpower after the
first week, and the first week is annoying, and that is the whole
cost.

## The photographs

One more habit belongs on this page because your child cannot set it for you.

Adults post their children publicly for years, then ask those same children to
be careful about what they share online. The correction is cheap: ask first,
and accept no. A four-year-old can hold this exchange, and [being asked is how
they learn to ask](/conversations/what-they-know-about-you).

## What to say

When your kid catches you at the table:

> "You're right. Same rule, my mistake. It's going in the hall."

No speech, no justification, and do not soften it with a laugh. Being caught and
folding instantly is the most instructive thing they will see a phone do all
week.

When you surface from twenty minutes of scrolling in front of them:

> "I've just lost twenty minutes and I couldn't tell you to what. I'm putting it
> away."

Narrating the exit teaches the exit. Getting pulled under never looks like much from the outside; showing a child what noticing looks like is rarer and more useful than never slipping.

When work needs you reachable:

> "I'm on call tonight, so my phone is out and I'll be checking it. That's the
> exception, and you can hold me to the difference."

## When this doesn't apply

Parents whose phone is the job: on call, running a business from it, the only
route to a co-parent or an ageing relative. The advice compresses instead of
vanishing. Notifications from the people who matter stay on and the rest go,
and narrate what the phone is doing when you pick it up, because "I'm answering
Grandma" and silent scrolling look identical from across the room and teach
opposite things.

A single parent coordinating an entire household through one device is not
failing this page. The fixed spot still helps, if only because it means the
coordination happens standing in the hall rather than woven through every
minute of the evening.
