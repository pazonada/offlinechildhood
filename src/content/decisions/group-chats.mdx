## Why this one is different

Every other decision on this site costs you an argument. This one costs your
child something, and it is worth being honest about that instead of talking around it.

When you say no to the group chat, your kid does miss things. Plans get made
there. Jokes happen there and get referenced the next day. The social world
of a twelve-year-old is being coordinated somewhere they are not. Telling them
that is not real, or that the friendships are not real, does not work, mostly
because it is not true, and they know it is not true.

So the honest position is that this decision has a price. What follows is about lowering that price, since pretending it away does not work.

There is a version of this worth knowing about from the other end of education.
When [MIT studied how its own students use AI](https://aiandeducation.mit.edu/report/), one of the findings that surprised
the committee was social: leaning on a chatbot displaced the study group, the
classmate and the office hour. The same substitution is available to a
twelve-year-old, and the thing being substituted for is the same thing.

## The price is set by other parents

Almost none of the cost comes from
being without an app. It comes from being the only one without it.

That makes this less a rule you set at home than a norm, and norms are set by
groups. Three families in a school year deciding together is
worth more than any amount of firmness in one household, and it converts your
kid's position from "the only one whose mum said no" to "one of the ones who
isn't on it yet". Those are entirely different social facts.

Nothing else on this site makes as much difference for as little effort, and
nothing else gets skipped as often, because sending that first message to other
parents feels awkward. Send it anyway. A surprising number of them are
waiting for someone else to go first. [Wait Until 8th](https://www.waituntil8th.org)
exists to make exactly this easier, and is worth borrowing from even if you just
do it informally over a WhatsApp thread.

## Solve the real need separately

Underneath "I want Snapchat" is usually something reasonable: I want to talk to
my friends without an adult arranging it.

That is a fair thing to want and it does not require a social platform. A basic
phone or a watch with a short approved contact list handles it. So does a shared
family device with messaging on it. What you are declining is the feed, the
public metrics, the disappearing content and the open door to strangers, and you
can decline all of that while still saying yes to talking to friends.

## What to say

When they say they are missing everything:

> "You are missing some of it. I'm not going to pretend you're not. Here's what
> I'm going to do about it."

Then actually do something: arrange the in-person thing, get them a way to
message the three people who matter, call the other parents.

When they ask when:

> "Year nine. I'm telling you now so it's a plan and not a fight."

A named year is far better than an indefinite no. It gives them something to hold
and it stops every request being a fresh negotiation.

When another parent says yes:

> "That's their call and I'm not judging it. Ours is different."

## When this doesn't apply

Older teenagers already inside these platforms are a different problem, and
removal at that stage usually costs more than it gains. The work there is about settings, sleep and honest conversation; prohibition at that stage costs more than it gets.

A kid whose entire social world is geographically distant, because of a move, or
family abroad, or a small rural community, has a different calculation.
The connection may be worth more than the cost. Choose the most boring tool that
does the job, and start the [conversations](/conversations) earlier than you
otherwise would.
