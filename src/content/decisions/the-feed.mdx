## The video is not the problem

Parents tend to worry about a specific video their child might see. That is worth
some attention, but it is not where the damage usually is.

The real issue is structural. When a video ends, a system decides what plays
next, and that system is measured on how long people keep watching. It is not
malicious and there is no person choosing. It is an optimiser doing exactly what
it was built to do, and what it discovers, over billions of attempts, is that
certain things hold attention better than others. More extreme is stickier than
moderate. Louder is stickier than quiet. A thing that makes you slightly anxious
is stickier than a thing that leaves you content.

Nobody designed that outcome. It emerged from the target, which is more
unsettling than a villain would be, and it is why arguing about individual videos
misses the point.

## Two settings do most of the work

Autoplay off. This one switch converts watching from something that continues by
default into something that requires a decision. It is the difference between a
child choosing to watch a fourth video and a child simply not stopping.

Notifications off. There is no version of a YouTube notification that serves your
child. Every one of them exists to restart a session that had ended.

That is genuinely most of it. No parental control software, no time limits, no
subscription. Two switches.

## What this looks like in daily life

Watch on the biggest screen in the house rather than the smallest. A television
in the living room is a social object. A phone under a duvet is not. Same
content, entirely different behaviour around it.

Use search and playlists rather than the home page. Make a playlist together for
things they actually like. It takes ten minutes and it removes the feed from the
equation without removing the videos.

Sit through some of it. Not all, and not as supervision. This is the richest
possible ground for the [conversations](/conversations), because the mechanism is
happening live in front of you and you can just point at it.

Do not rely on the kids' version of the app to solve this. It is better than
nothing, and it is still a recommendation feed with the same incentives.

## What to say

When you turn autoplay off:

> "I turned off the bit that picks the next one. You can still pick anything you
> want. I'd just rather you were the one picking."

When they land somewhere strange:

> "How did you end up here? Let's trace it back."

Do that without any annoyance in your voice and you get two things: a genuinely
interesting archaeology lesson, and a kid who tells you next time.

When they say it is their favourite thing:

> "I know. I'm not taking it away. I'm taking away the part that decides for you."

## When this doesn't apply

Video is a real interest for a lot of kids, and some of them are learning to make
it. A child studying editing, or animation, or how a particular creator builds a
story, is doing something worth encouraging. The feed is still the feed, but the
watching has a point, and treating it as the same activity as idle scrolling will
land as unfair because it is.

Households where a screen genuinely buys a parent the twenty minutes needed to
cook dinner are not failing at anything. Autoplay off, playlist on, and let it
go.
