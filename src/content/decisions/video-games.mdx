## Sort games by who pays

"Video games" covers things with almost nothing in common. Lumping them together
is why so many conversations about them go nowhere.

Some games you buy once. The people who made it were paid when you bought it, and
they have no further interest in how long you play. These games end. They have
credits.

Other games are free, or nearly, and make money continuously while you play,
through cosmetics, battle passes, loot boxes and currency. These games are
designed by people whose job is to keep sessions going and bring you back
tomorrow. Daily rewards, streaks that break if you miss a day, limited-time
events that end on Sunday. None of that is there for your child's benefit.

That distinction will serve you better than any age rating. A ten-year-old
playing a bought-once puzzle game for ninety minutes is fine. The same child
spending twenty minutes a day maintaining a streak is being farmed.

## What games are actually good for

We are not against this. Games do things worth having.

They are one of the few places left where children fail repeatedly at something
difficult and keep going. That is hard to arrange elsewhere, and it is
worth a lot.

They are also social infrastructure now. For many kids, playing together online
is how friendship happens on a wet Tuesday, and cutting it off can be a real
social cost rather than an imaginary one. Take that seriously when you weigh it.

Some are creative tools in disguise. Building, designing, problem-solving,
occasionally programming. A kid who makes things in a game is doing something
much closer to model-building than to watching television.

## What this looks like in daily life

Turn off in-app purchases at the account level, where one setting covers every game at once. This takes
five minutes and removes the entire economic engine behind the compulsive design.

Look at what they play with them, once, properly. Ask them to show you. You will
learn more in twenty minutes of being shown a game than in a year of reading
about it, and it lands very differently from an inspection.

Prefer stopping points to timers. "Finish the level" respects the thing that
makes games satisfying. A timer cutting off mid-match creates a genuine grievance
and teaches nothing.

Voice chat with strangers is a different decision from gaming. You can allow one
and not the other, and most parents who think they have allowed a game have
actually allowed an open channel to adults they have never met. That decision has
a page of its own, under [the people in the
game](/decisions/the-people-in-the-game), along with the reason the in-app
purchase setting matters for more than your bank balance.

## What to say

When you want to understand what they are playing:

> "Show me. I want to see what's good about it."

When a game has a daily streak:

> "See how it punishes you for missing a day? That's not part of the game. That's
> the part that's there to make sure you come back."

When time is up mid-match:

> "Finish the round. Then we're done."

Then hold it, because you have just made a reasonable deal and the whole thing
depends on both sides keeping it.

## When this doesn't apply

For some kids, particularly those who find in-person socialising hard, the
game is where the friendship actually lives. Be
very careful about removing that, and think about what replaces it before you do.

Competitive play at a serious level is its own thing, closer to a sport than a
pastime, and it deserves the same conversation you would have about any demanding commitment, with no special case made for this one.
