## Boredom is not the enemy

The first twenty minutes after a screen goes off are unpleasant. Your child will
be at a loose end and will tell you so, repeatedly, and the temptation to solve
it is enormous.

Do not solve it. That flat, itchy, nothing-to-do feeling is the raw material for
everything that comes next. A child who is never bored never has to invent
anything, because something is always being provided. The invention only starts
once the provision stops, and it takes longer than you would like.

This is also why swapping a screen for a packed schedule does not work. Both fill
the time. Only one of them leaves room for the child to do the filling.

## What the evidence actually supports

We are careful elsewhere on this site about overclaiming, so it is worth saying
that this is the part where the research is strongest, and it is positive rather
than negative.

Time in unstructured free play in the early years predicted stronger
self-regulation years later, in a large Australian study following children over
time. Self-regulation is not a small outcome. It tracks with health, education
and financial outcomes across a lifetime.

Outdoor play that involves some real risk, climbing higher than is entirely
comfortable, going a bit further than usual, has a formal position statement from
the Canadian Paediatric Society behind it. Their conclusion is that it supports
physical activity, social competence, risk perception and self-esteem, and that
over-restricting it carries its own costs. Keeping children safe from everything
is not a neutral choice.

Notice that none of this is an argument about screens. It is an argument about
what the time is for.

## What this looks like on a Tuesday

Send them outside without a plan. Not to a park with equipment and a scheduled
end, just outside. This feels like neglect the first few times and it is not.

Buy the waterproofs. British and Pacific Northwest parents already know this: the
weather stops being a factor the moment the clothing is right, and a huge fraction
of the outdoor time other families lose is lost to not owning a coat.

Make it social. One other child transforms an empty afternoon, and it is far
easier to arrange than a class.

Leave the ingredients lying around. Cardboard, tape, string, a torch, a magnifying
glass, cheap tools, a camera with actual film in it. Objects that do one thing
badly invite more invention than objects that do everything well.

Have somewhere to put the results. A shelf, a wall, a windowsill. Making things
matters more when the things persist.

## What to say

When they say they are bored:

> "Good. That's allowed."

Then go back to what you were doing. This is much harder than it sounds and it is
the whole technique.

When you are tempted to sign them up for another activity:

Ask yourself whether they asked for it. If the honest answer is that it fills a
gap you found uncomfortable, leave the gap.

## When this doesn't apply

Roaming freely depends enormously on where you live. A busy road with no
crossing, or a genuinely unsafe area, is not a mindset problem to be overcome.
The principle still holds, but it might mean a garden, a yard, a courtyard or a
regular trip somewhere rather than the front door.

Some children, particularly those who are autistic or have sensory differences,
do not experience unstructured time as freeing at all. It can be genuinely
distressing. Structure is not the enemy there. Adjust the shape of this: less
open-ended, more predictable, still not a screen.
