## Why this one first

Most of what parents argue about here is uncertain. This part is not.

When researchers looked at what actually drives the relationship between screens
and children's sleep, the answer was portable devices
specifically, the ones a kid can carry into a bedroom and into the bed itself. In a
population sample of American children from birth to seventeen, that was where
the effect lived.

Think about what a phone in a bedroom actually is. It is a device engineered by
very capable people to be difficult to put down, handed to a tired child, alone,
in the dark, with nobody watching and no natural stopping point. Of course it
wins. It would win against us.

And sleep is upstream of everything else on your list. Attention, mood, patience,
appetite, the ability to have a reasonable conversation about screen time at all.
A tired child is worse at every single thing you are trying to help them with.

## What this looks like in daily life

Buy a cheap multi-port charger. Put it in the kitchen or the hallway, somewhere
public and slightly inconvenient. Everything charges there overnight.

Buy each kid an actual alarm clock, about ten pounds or dollars. This removes the
only reasonable objection anyone will raise, and it removes it for the
price of a takeaway.

Your phone goes on the charger too. This is the part people skip, and it is the
part that makes the whole thing work. A rule that applies to the children and not
to the adults is a rule your children will spend the next decade litigating, and
they will be right to.

If a kid needs audio to fall asleep, that is fair, and it does not require a
screen. A Yoto or a Toniebox plays stories and music with no display, no feed and
no way to end up somewhere else at eleven at night.

## What to say

When they ask why:

> "Because everyone in this house sleeps better without a screen in the room, me
> included. That's why mine is out here too."

When they say they only use it as an alarm:

> "Fair. Here's an alarm clock."

When they say they will not look at it:

> "I believe you mean that. I also don't look at mine, right up until I do. It's
> not a willpower thing. The thing is built to be hard to put down."

When you are the one breaking the rule:

> "You're right, I had my phone upstairs last night. Same rule; I broke
> it."

## When this doesn't apply

A kid who needs a device for a medical alert, or to reach a parent they do not
live with, or as an accessibility tool. In those cases the device stays, and the
rest still applies: it charges across the room rather than under the pillow, and
notifications go off at night.

Teenagers who have had a phone in the bedroom for years are a harder case, and
pulling it out overnight will feel to them like a punishment for something they
have not done. Start by making the change together with a reason attached instead of announcing it. If that fails, negotiate the charger being across the room
before you negotiate it leaving. Distance is most of the benefit.
